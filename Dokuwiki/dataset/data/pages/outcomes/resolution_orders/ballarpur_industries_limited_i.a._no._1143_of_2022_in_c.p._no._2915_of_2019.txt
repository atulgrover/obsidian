### Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016: A Study Guide

**Short Answer Questions**

1. **What are the grounds on which an application can be filed under Section 7 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016?**  
   An application under Section 7 of the IBC can be filed by a financial creditor against a corporate debtor who has committed a default in repaying a financial debt. The application must establish the existence of a debt and the occurrence of a default.

2. **Explain the concept of a 'Financial Creditor' under the IBC 2016. What distinguishes them from other types of creditors?**  
   A Financial Creditor, as defined under Section 5(8) of the IBC, is a person or entity to whom a financial debt is owed. They are typically involved in the financial operations of the corporate debtor, having assessed their viability and potentially engaged in loan restructuring. Unlike operational creditors, their relationship with the debtor is primarily financial.

3. **What is the purpose of a 'Resolution Plan' under the IBC? How is it approved and implemented?**  
   A Resolution Plan is a proposal presented during the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process that aims to revive the financially distressed corporate debtor. It outlines the restructuring of debts, potential changes in business operations, and other measures to restore the company's financial health. A resolution plan must be approved by the Committee of Creditors (CoC) and subsequently by the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT).

4. **What is the role of the Resolution Professional in a Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process?**  
   The Resolution Professional acts as an interim manager of the corporate debtor during the CIRP. They are responsible for collecting information about the company's assets and liabilities, verifying claims submitted by creditors, managing the company's affairs, and facilitating the formulation and approval of a resolution plan.

5. **Can the security interest of a third party (who is not the Corporate Debtor) be modified or extinguished through a Resolution Plan? Explain with reference to the Jaypee Kensington Boulevard Apartments case.**  
   The Supreme Court, in the Jaypee Kensington Boulevard Apartments case, held that a third-party security interest cannot be extinguished or modified through a resolution plan unless it is specifically considered during the CIRP and provisions for its treatment are clearly outlined in the plan. The court emphasized that such security interests cannot be annulled solely at the discretion of the resolution applicant.

6. **What are the implications of the "maxim vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt" in the context of claims submission under IBC?**  
   The maxim "vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt" means "the law assists those who are vigilant, not those who sleep on their rights." In the context of IBC, it emphasizes the importance of timely action by creditors in submitting their claims. Delayed or belated claims may be rejected if the creditor fails to demonstrate sufficient cause for the delay.

7. **Under what circumstances can the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) admit a belated claim in an insolvency resolution process?**  
   The NCLT may admit a belated claim in an insolvency resolution process if the creditor can provide sufficient justification for the delay in submission. The justification should demonstrate that the delay was due to circumstances beyond the creditor's control and that admitting the claim would not prejudice other stakeholders or disrupt the resolution process.

8. **Briefly explain the grounds on which the NCLT can reject a resolution plan.**  
   The NCLT can reject a resolution plan if it violates any provisions of the IBC, is not in the best interests of the corporate debtor, or is not feasible to implement. The plan must also comply with applicable laws and regulations and ensure fair treatment of all stakeholders.

9. **What are the different classes of creditors under IBC and how does their treatment differ in a resolution plan?**  
   The IBC classifies creditors into Financial Creditors, Operational Creditors, and other creditors. Financial creditors have claims related to financial debts, while operational creditors have claims related to the provision of goods or services. The treatment of each class differs in a resolution plan, with financial creditors typically having greater influence in the decision-making process.

10. **What are the roles of the Adjudicating Authority and the Appellate Tribunal under the IBC?**  
    The Adjudicating Authority under IBC, usually the NCLT, has the power to initiate the CIRP, approve or reject resolution plans, and pass orders related to the insolvency resolution process. The Appellate Tribunal, the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT), hears appeals against the orders of the Adjudicating Authority.

| Financial Creditor | A creditor whose claim arises from a financial debt owed by the corporate debtor. |

| National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) | The appellate body that hears appeals against the orders of the NCLT. |
| Moratorium | A period during the CIRP where legal actions against the corporate debtor are suspended to allow for the resolution process to proceed. |

| Section 29A | A section of the IBC that specifies the eligibility criteria for resolution applicants to prevent those with a history of financial misconduct from participating in the resolution process. |

### Briefing Doc: Review of Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) Cases in India

This briefing document analyzes several orders and judgments related to the application of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) in India. The primary themes include:

1. **Admissibility of Claims and Time Limitations:**

   **Case:** M/s Finquest Financial Solutions Ltd. vs. Ballabpur Industries Limited  
   **Key Fact:** The Applicant sought directions for the Resolution Professional (RP) to admit their belated claim.  
   **Outcome:** The application was rejected.  
   **Reasoning:** The Applicant failed to provide a valid reason for the delay and the maxim "vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt" (law assists those who are vigilant, not those who sleep over their rights) was applied. The Tribunal highlighted the importance of concluding the insolvency process in a timely manner.  
   **Quote:** “The Applicant has been sleeping over its statutory rights. The Applicant has woken up after the 2nd Plan has been approved by the CoC on 14.04.2022.”

2. **Treatment of Third-Party Security Interests:**

   **Case:** Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Company Ltd. vs. Corporate Debtor Shree Gopal Industries Limited  
   **Key Fact:** Edelweiss held security interest over land belonging to the Corporate Debtor, offered as security for a loan taken by a related company (BGPPL). BGPPL hadn't defaulted on the loan.  
   **Outcome:** Edelweiss's application to enforce its security interest outside the CIRP was rejected.  
   **Reasoning:** While the Supreme Court in Jaypee Kensington affirmed that third-party security interests can't be extinguished arbitrarily, this case differed. The RP had assessed and admitted Edelweiss's claim at a notional value, placing them under "Other Creditors/ Secured creditor". This implied the security interest was considered during the CIRP.  
   **Quote:** “Considering the above, it emerges that a Security Interest which has duly been included/ considered as a part of CIRP can be specifically dealt with under the resolution plan without seeking any consent from the relevant Secured Creditor.”

3. **Distinction between Financial and Secured Creditors:**

   **Case:** M/s Finquest Financial Solutions Private Limited vs. Ballabpur Industries Limited  
   **Key Fact:** The court emphasized the unique position of financial creditors as opposed to mere secured creditors.  
   **Reasoning:** A financial creditor has a more involved relationship with the corporate debtor, participating in the company's operations and financial restructuring. This distinguishes them from secured creditors who merely hold security against a debt.  
   **Quote:** “What is intended by the expression ‘financial creditor’ is a person who has direct engagement in the functioning of the corporate debtor; who is involved right from the beginning while assessing the viability of the corporate debtor.”

4. **Approval and Implementation of Resolution Plans:**

   **Case:** Shree Gopal Industries Limited  
   **Key Fact:** The Resolution Professional sought approval for a resolution plan.  
   **Outcome:** The application was allowed and the plan approved.  
   **Details:** The plan involved a complex structure of debt restructuring, equity issuance, and treatment of various liabilities. It also provided a detailed timeline for implementation and addressed the management of the Corporate Debtor.

### Important Takeaways:

- **Timely filing of claims is crucial:** Creditors must adhere to the prescribed timelines for submitting their claims. Delays can lead to rejection, even if the claim is otherwise valid.
- **Third-party security interests require careful consideration:** While the IBC allows for modification or extinguishment of security interests, the rights of third parties who are not part of the CIRP must be protected.
- **Financial Creditors hold a special position:** The IBC recognizes the distinct role played by financial creditors and grants them greater influence in the CIRP.
- **Resolution Plans must be comprehensive and feasible:** Approved plans need to clearly address debt restructuring, equity allocation, treatment of liabilities, management changes, and implementation timelines.

Overall, these cases demonstrate the evolving application of the IBC in India and highlight the complexities involved in resolving corporate insolvency.

### FAQ: Corporate Debtor and Secured Creditor Relationship

1. **What happens when a Corporate Debtor provides security for a loan taken by its affiliate company, and the Corporate Debtor enters into insolvency proceedings?**  
   When a Corporate Debtor provides security for a loan taken by its affiliate, and the Corporate Debtor itself faces insolvency, the fate of the secured asset is determined by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) and its regulations. The secured creditor, although not a direct creditor of the Corporate Debtor, has a significant interest in the secured asset.

2. **Can the security interest of a third party, such as a secured creditor of an affiliate, be extinguished through the resolution plan of the Corporate Debtor?**  
   The extinguishment of a third party's security interest is a complex issue with differing legal interpretations. The Supreme Court, in the *Jaypee Kensington Boulevard Apartments Welfare Association & Ors. vs. NBCC (India) Ltd. & Ors.* case, provided guidance on this matter. The court suggested that if the security interest is not directly part of the Corporate Debtor's resolution process, it cannot be simply extinguished based on the resolution applicant's decision. However, if the security interest is duly considered during the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) and the resolution plan includes a specific provision addressing its treatment, it can be dealt with without necessarily requiring the secured creditor's consent.

3. **What happens if the third-party, whose security interest is being affected by the resolution plan, is not a part of the CIRP?**  
   In cases where the third-party security provider is not a part of the CIRP, extinguishing their security interest through the Corporate Debtor's resolution plan is controversial. According to the Supreme Court's observation in the *Jaypee* case, if a security interest is not included in the CIRP, it cannot be extinguished based solely on the resolution applicant's proposal.

4. **Can the resolution plan modify or deal with the security interest of a secured creditor even if the principal borrower (affiliate company) has not defaulted on the loan?**  
   Yes, under Regulation 37 of the IBBI (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016, a resolution plan can propose the modification or satisfaction of any security interest related to the Corporate Debtor. This provision does not explicitly require the principal borrower to be in default for such actions. However, practical considerations and legal precedents suggest that extinguishing a third-party security interest without their involvement or default by the principal borrower may be challenged.

5. **Does the IBC provide a specific timeframe for filing claims during the CIRP?**  
   Yes, while the IBC does not explicitly specify a strict deadline for claim submission, it emphasizes the importance of timely action. Creditors are expected to file their claims promptly after the initiation of CIRP. Delays in filing claims may lead to rejection, especially if the delay affects the resolution process's timeline. The legal principle of "vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt" (the law assists those who are vigilant, not those who sleep upon their rights) often applies in such situations.

6. **Can a creditor who failed to submit their claim within the CIRP timeline approach the Adjudicating Authority later?**  
   The IBC prioritizes a time-bound resolution process for Corporate Debtors. Allowing creditors to file claims belatedly can disrupt this objective. While there might be exceptional circumstances where courts permit belated claims, generally, creditors are expected to adhere to the CIRP timelines. Entertaining belated claims after the approval of a resolution plan would undermine the IBC's purpose of providing a swift and efficient resolution mechanism.

7. **What is the role of the Resolution Professional (RP) in handling claims and security interests during the CIRP?**  
   The RP plays a crucial role in managing the CIRP, including handling claims and evaluating security interests. They are responsible for:
   - Collecting and verifying claims submitted by creditors.
   - Assessing the validity and value of security interests.
   - Determining the order of priority for claim payments based on the IBC's waterfall mechanism.
   - Preparing the information memorandum and inviting resolution plans from potential resolution applicants.
   - Presenting the resolution plans to the Committee of Creditors (CoC) for voting.

8. **What are the key takeaways from the "Satyaprakash Aggarwal vs. Vistara Metal Industries (P) Ltd." case concerning the extinguishing of security interests?**  
   This case highlights that extinguishing a third-party security interest without their involvement and without the principal borrower's default is a complex legal matter. The NCLT, while upholding the resolution plan, acknowledged the differing legal perspectives on this issue. It emphasized the importance of a well-defined framework to address such situations in future cases.
