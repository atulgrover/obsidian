## Summary
Vinit Nagar, the speaker, will summarize recent orders from the NT and NCL High Court, as well as certain past orders related to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC). He will discuss cases one by one, sharing the facts, issues of law, and disposal of each case. The first case he will discuss is SVA Family Welfare Trust versus UAS Energy, where the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) held that a resolution plan can contain a clause extinguishing the security interest of a financial creditor, such as a personal guarantee, after paying compensation to the financial creditor. The NCLAT observed that once a resolution plan has been accepted by the Committee of Creditors (COC) with the requisite majority, the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) cannot sit over the commercial wisdom of the COC. The second case is Bank of Baroda versus SVA, where the National Company Law Tribunal (NCTL) rejected the resolution plan submitted by SVA on the grounds that it contained a provision for extinguishment of personal guarantee, which is not permitted by the IBC. However, the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) set aside the order passed by the NCTL and held that a resolution plan can contain a clause extinguishing the security interest of a financial creditor by way of personal guarantee. The NCLAT observed that the approval of the plan does not discharge the guarantor, and the plan may contain a clause extinguishing the personal guarantee, provided the secured creditor is compensated for the same. The NCLAT further held that the commercial wisdom of the COC in acceptance of the same should not be impeded at the instance of a dissenting financial creditor. The Supreme Court upheld the order passed by the NCLAT. The third case is Puro Naturals JV versus Warana Shahari Bank and Others, where the NCLAT held that the resolution plan providing for extinguishment of the personal guarantee as approved by the COC does not contravene any provisions of the IBC. The NCLAT further observed that in the event of liquidation of the corporate debtor, the payment to a dissenting financial creditor is entitled for payment in accordance with Section 53I, and there is nothing in the IBC that mandates the applicant to make a front payment to the dissenting creditors. The payment to dissenting financial creditors is given priority, and the focus is on priority and not on the upfront payment.
