<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">26. <p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">27. There the Supme Court has answered the question in negative. Reliance is also placed on the decision in BPL Ltd. v. R. Sudhakar (2004) 7 SCC 219.</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">28. This Court has considered the above submissions and in particular the observations of the Supme Court in <a href="/doc/160831025/">Common Cause</a> (supra). The answer perhaps lies in a recent decision of the Supme Court in <a href="/doc/30560910/">Ghanashyam Mishra and Sons Private Ltd. v. Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Company Ltd.</a> 2021 SCC Online SC 313. There the Supme Court considered all of the earlier decisions including <a href="/doc/7427609/">Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel</a> W.P.(C) No.20286 of 2020 Page 16 of 21 (supra), <a href="/doc/5839676/">Maharashtra Seamless Limited v. Padmanabhan Venkatesh</a> (<a href="/doc/181931435/">2020) 11 SCC 467 and Innovative Industries v. ICICI Bank</a> (2018) 1 SCC 407. The Supme Court also referred to its earlier decision in <a href="/doc/28868630/">Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank</a> (2019) 12 SCC 150. After discussing all of the above judgments, the Supme Court in <a href="/doc/30560910/">Ghanashyam Mishra and Sons Private Ltd.</a> (supra) held as under:</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis">"57. It could thus be seen, that the legislature has given paramount importance to the commercial wisdom of CoC and the scope of judicial review by Adjudicating Authority is limited to the extent provided under <a href="/doc/136184431/">Section 31</a> of I&amp;B Code and of the Appellate Authority is limited to the extent provided under subsection (3) of Section 61 of the I&amp;B Code, is no more res integra.</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis">58. Bare reading of <a href="/doc/136184431/">Section 31</a> of the I&amp;B Code would also make it abundantly clear, that once the resolution plan is approved by the Adjudicating Authority, after it is satisfied, that the resolution plan as approved by CoC meets the requirements as referred to in subsection (2) of <a href="/doc/92325758/">Section 30</a>, it shall be binding on the Corporate Debtor and its employees, members, creditors, guarantors and other stakeholders. Such a provision is necessitated since one of the dominant purposes of the I&amp;B Code is, revival of the Corporate Debtor and to make it a running concern.</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis">59. The resolution plan submitted by successful resolution applicant is required to contain various provisions, viz., provision for payment of insolvency resolution process costs, provision for payment of debts of operational creditors, which shall not be less than the amount to be paid to such creditors in the event of liquidation of the Corporate Debtor under section 53; or the amount that would W.P.(C) No.20286 of 2020 Page 17 of 21 have been paid to such creditors, if the amount to be distributed under the resolution plan had been distributed in accordance with the order of priority in subsection (1) of section 53, whichever is higher. The resolution plan is also required to provide for the payment of debts of financial creditors, who do not vote in favour of the resolution plan, which also shall not be less than the amount to be paid to such creditors in accordance with sub section (1) of section 53 in the event of a liquidation of the Corporate Debtor. Explanation 1 to clause (b) of sub section (2) of <a href="/doc/92325758/">Section 30</a> of the I&amp;B Code clarifies for the removal of doubts, that a distribution in accordance with the provisions of the said clause shall be fair and equitable to such creditors. The resolution plan is also required to provide for the management of the affairs of the Corporate Debtor after approval of the resolution plan and also the implementation and supervision of the resolution plan. Clause (e) of subsection (2) of <a href="/doc/92325758/">Section 30</a> of I&amp;B Code also casts a duty on RP to examine, that the resolution plan does not contravene any of the provisions of the law for the time being in force.</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis">60. Perusal of <a href="/doc/111363155/">Section 29</a> of the I&amp;B Code read with Regulation 36 of the Regulations would reveal, that it requires RP to ppare an information memorandum containing various details of the Corporate Debtor so that the resolution applicant submitting a plan is aware of the assets and liabilities of the Corporate Debtor, including the details about the creditors and the amounts claimed by them. It is also required to contain the details of guarantees that have been given in relation to the debts of the corporate debtor by other persons. The details with regard to all material litigation and an ongoing investigation or proceeding initiated by Government and statutory authorities are also required to be contained in the information W.P.(C) No.20286 of 2020 Page 18 of 21 memorandum. So also the details regarding the number of workers and employees and liabilities of the Corporate Debtor towards them are required to be contained in the information memorandum.</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis">61. All these details are required to be contained in the information memorandum so that the resolution applicant is aware, as to what are the liabilities that he may have to face and provide for a plan, which apart from satisfying a part of such liabilities would also ensure, that the Corporate Debtor is revived and made a running establishment. The legislative intent of making the resolution plan binding on all the stakeholders after it gets the seal of approval from the Adjudicating Authority upon its satisfaction, that the resolution plan approved by CoC meets the requirement as referred to in sub- section (2) of <a href="/doc/92325758/">Section 30</a> is, that after the approval of the resolution plan, no surprise claims should be flung on the successful resolution applicant. The dominant purpose is, that he should start with fresh slate on the basis of the resolution plan approved."</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">29. The above observations in <a href="/doc/30560910/">Ghanashyam Mishra and Sons Private Ltd.</a> (supra), are also complete answer to the plea of Mr. Muduli, learned AGA that the dues arising out of the judgment of the Supme Court in Common Cause were not specifically dealt with in the ARP. As pointed out in <a href="/doc/30560910/">Ghanashyam Mishra</a> (supra) "after the approval of the resolution plan, no surprise claims should be flung on the successful resolution applicant". Further, the resolution Applicant "should start with fresh slate on the basis of the resolution plan approved".</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">30. The decision in <a href="/doc/18700540/">Association of Unified Telecom Services Providers</a> (supra), was obviously in the peculiar facts and circumstances of that case. A careful perusal of the judgment reveals that the particular W.P.(C) No.20286 of 2020 Page 19 of 21 question whether "deferred/default payment of instalments of spectrum acquisition cost can be termed as operational dues besides AGR dues"</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis">was left to be considered first by the NCLT. In other words, the question whether the licencee companies before the NCLT under the IBC were liable for the AGR dues was left to the decision of the NCLT. The Supme Court made it clear that "we have not observed on the merits of the case, and we have kept all the questions open to be examined by the NCLT".</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">31. The decision in <a href="/doc/894802/">BPL Ltd. V. R. Sudhakar</a> (supra) is also distinguishable since it did not involve the effect of the ARP under the IBC after its approval by both the NCLT and the NCLAT. With the IBC a different legal regime has been put in place and which has been lucidly explained in several decisions of the Supme Court as noted hereinbefore.</p>
<p title="Conclusion" data-structure="Conclusion">32. In terms of <a href="/doc/136184431/">Section 31</a> of the IBC, the ARP is binding on all creditors including Central Government and the State Government. Since all of the impugned demands raised against FACOR pertain to the period prior to the Plan Effective date i.e. 31st January, 2020, all such demands stand automatically extinguished in terms of the ARP.</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">33. In that view of the matter, the impugned demand raised against the Petitioner by the Opposite Parties on the strength of the decision of the Supme Court in <a href="/doc/160831025/">Common Cause</a> (supra) are unsustainable in law and are hereby set aside. Consequently, a direction is issued to the Opposite W.P.(C) No.20286 of 2020 Page 20 of 21 Parties to refund the amounts paid by the Petitioner under protest for the purpose of issuance of the MDCC and renewal of the trading licence.</p>
<p title="Conclusion" data-structure="Conclusion">34. For the reasons explained hereinbefore, the following directions are issued:</p>
<p title="Conclusion">(a) the demand notices set out in para 13 (z) of the petition stands quashed;</p>
<p title="Conclusion">(b) with the quashing of the impugned demand notices, a direction is issued to the Opposite Parties either to refund to the Petitioner the amounts paid by it under protest or adjust the amount so paid in the sum of Rs.12,02,28,202/- against the dues payable by it to the Opposite Parties in future against undisputed amounts.</p>
<p title="Conclusion" data-structure="Conclusion">35. The writ petition is disposed of in the above terms, but in the circumstances, with no order as to costs.</p>
