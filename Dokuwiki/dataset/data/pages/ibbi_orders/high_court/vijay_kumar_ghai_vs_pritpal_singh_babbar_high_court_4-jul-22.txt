<p title="Issue" data-structure="Issue">1. Vide this petition, the petitioner challenges by way of invoking jurisdiction of this court under <a href="/doc/1679850/">Section 482</a> of the Cr.P.C., the order passed by the learned JMIC, Jalandhar, dated 25.05.2021 (copy Annexure P-23), by which his application seeking a stay on the proceedings initiated under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, was dismissed; with that court holding that simply because the petitioner had filed an application under <a href="/doc/992144/">Section 94</a> of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (in short "the IBC"</p>
<p title="Issue">or the "Code"), that would not mean that the proceedings under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> would get automatically stayed even in terms of <a href="/doc/63256/">Section 96</a> of the said Code, in view of the fact that the cheque in question was issued by the petitioner in his personal capacity and was not in any manner in discharge of any corporate debt in respect of 'his company'.</p>
<p title="Issue">Thus the entire controversy is as to whether criminal 1 of 48 ::: Downloaded on - 09-07-2022 21:04:16 ::: CRM-M-22685-2021 (O&amp;M) -2- proceedings under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, (hereinafter referred to as the "<a href="/doc/1132672/">NI Act</a>" or the "Act"), would also remain stayed in terms of <a href="/doc/63256/">Section 96</a> of the Code, even where the cheque in question was not issued to discharge a 'corporate debt', though issued by a personal guarantor qua a corporate debtor, but is not a cheque qua parties as are adversaries or litigants in any proceedings before the National Company Law Tribunal/Resolution Professional/Interim Resolution Professional.</p>

<p title="Issue">"11. As is discernible, the Preamble gives an insight into what is sought to be achieved by the Code. The Code is first and foremost, a Code for reorganization and insolvency resolution of corporate debtors. Unless such reorganization is effected in a time-bound manner, the value of the assets of such persons will deplete. Therefore, maximization of value of the assets of such persons so that they are efficiently run as going concerns is another very important objective of the Code. This, in turn, will promote entrepneurship as the persons in management of the corporate debtor are removed and replaced by entrepneurs. When, therefore, a resolution plan takes off and the corporate debtor is brought back into the economic mainstream, it is able to repay its debts, which, in turn, enhances the viability of credit in the hands of banks and financial institutions. Above all, ultimately, the interests of all stakeholders are looked after as the corporate debtor itself becomes a beneficiary of the resolution scheme - workers are paid, the creditors in the long run will be repaid in full, and shareholders/investors are able to maximize their investment. Timely resolution of a corporate debtor who is in the red, by an effective legal framework, would go a long way to support the development of credit markets. Since more investment can be made with funds that have come back into the economy, business then eases up, which leads, overall, to higher economic growth and development of the Indian economy. What is interesting to note is that the Preamble does not, in any manner, refer to liquidation, which is only availed of as a last resort if there is either no resolution plan or the resolution plans submitted are not up to the mark. <p title="Issue">12. It can thus be seen that the primary focus of the legislation is to ensure revival and continuation of the corporate debtor by protecting the corporate debtor from its own management and from a corporate death by liquidation. The Code is thus a beneficial legislation which puts the corporate debtor back on its feet, not being a mere recovery legislation for creditors. The interests of the corporate debtor have, therefore, been bifurcated and separated from 8 of 48 ::: Downloaded on - 09-07-2022 21:04:17 ::: CRM-M-22685-2021 (O&amp;M) -9- that of its promoters / those who are in management. The moratorium imposed by <a href="/doc/173961/">Section 14</a> is in the interest of the corporate debtor itself, thereby pserving the assets of the corporate debtor during the resolution process. The timelines within which the resolution process is to take place again protects the corporate debtor's assets from further dilution, and also protects all its creditors and workers by seeing that the resolution process goes through as fast as possible so that another management can, through its entrepneurial skills, resuscitate the corporate debtor to achieve all these ends."</p>

<p title="Analysis of the law" data-structure="Section">95. The impugned notification authorises the Central Government and the Board to frame rules and regulations on how to allow the pending actions against a personal guarantor to a corporate debtor before the Adjudicating Authority. The intent of the notification, facially, is to allow for pending proceedings to be adjudicated in terms of the Code. Section 243, which provides for the repeal of the personal insolvency laws has not as yet been notified. <a href="/doc/146441/">Section 60(2)</a> pscribes that in the event of an ongoing resolution process or liquidation process against a corporate debtor, an application for resolution process or bankruptcy of the personal guarantor to the corporate debtor shall be filed with the concerned NCLT seized of the resolution process or liquidation. Therefore, the Adjudicating Authority for personal guarantors will be the NCLT, if a parallel resolution process or liquidation process is pending in respect of a corporate debtor for whom the guarantee is given. The same logic pvails, under <a href="/doc/146441/">Section 60(3)</a>, when any insolvency or bankruptcy proceeding pending against the personal guarantor in a court or tribunal and a resolution process or liquidation is initiated against the corporate debtor. Thus if A, an individual is the subject of a resolution process before the DRT and he has furnished a personal guarantee for a debt owed by a company B, in the event a resolution process is initiated against B in an NCLT, the provision results in transferring the proceedings going on against A in the DRT to NCLT.</p>
<p title="Analysis of the law" data-structure="Section">96. This court in V. Ramakrishnan (supra), noticed why an application under <a href="/doc/146441/">Section 60(2)</a> could not be allowed. At that stage, neither Part III of the Code nor Section 243 had not been notified. This 11 of 48 ::: Downloaded on - 09-07-2022 21:04:17 ::: CRM-M-22685-2021 (O&amp;M) -12- meant that proceedings against personal guarantors stood outside the NCLT and the Code. The non-obstante provision under Section 238 gives the Code overriding effect over other pvailing enactments. This is perhaps the rationale for not notifying Section 243 as far as personal guarantors to corporate persons are concerned. Section 243 (2) saves pending proceedings under the Acts repealed (PIA and PTI Act) to be undertaken in accordance with those enactments. As of now, Section 243 has not been notified. In the event Section 243 is notified and those two Acts repealed, then, the psent notification would not have had the effect of covering pending proceedings against individuals, such as personal guarantors in other forums, and would bring them under the provisions of the Code pertaining to insolvency and bankruptcy of personal guarantors. The impugned notification, as a consequence of the non obstante clause in Section 238, has the result that if any proceeding were to be initiated against personal guarantors it would be under the Code."</p>

<p title="Petitioner's Argument" data-structure="PetArg">From that judgment, other than the paragraphs specifically referred to by learned counsel for the petitioner, what needs to be referred to by this court is that part of paragraph 86 as reproduces sub-section (2) of <a href="/doc/146441/">Section 60</a> of the Code, with that provision again being reproduced here, by highlighting what is considered necessary by this court for the purpose of the psent petition:-</p>
<p title="Petitioner's Argument">"86. XXXXX XXXXX XXXXX The amended <a href="/doc/146441/">Section 60(2)</a> reads as follows:-</p>
<p title="Petitioner's Argument">"(2) Without pjudice to sub-section (1) and notwithstanding anything to the contrary contained in this Code, where a corporate insolvency resolution process or liquidation proceeding of a corporate debtor is pending before a National Company Law Tribunal, an application relating to the insolvency resolution or liquidation or bankruptcy of a corporate guarantor or personal guarantor, as the case may be, of such corporate debtor shall be filed before the National Company Law Tribunal."</p>
<p title="Conclusion" data-structure="Conclusion">39 of 48 ::: Downloaded on - 09-07-2022 21:04:17 ::: CRM-M-22685-2021 (O&amp;M) -40-</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">33. Thus, even after the amendment of 2018 in the Code, sub-section (2) of <a href="/doc/146441/">Section 60</a> effectively states (even in terms of sub-section (1) thereof) that an application relating to the insolvency resolution or bankruptcy of a corporate guarantor or a personal guarantor, shall be filed before the NCLT.</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">Further, any application filed by a personal guarantor to a corporate debtor can only be filed if a corporate insolvency resolution process or liquidation proceeding of a corporate debtor is pending before the NCLT.</p>
<p title="Analysis of the law" data-structure="Section">In other words, a plain reading of the aforesaid provision would show that a personal guarantor to a corporate debtor cannot independently seek initiation of insolvency or bankruptcy etc. proceedings even before the NCLT in terms of sub-section (1) of <a href="/doc/146441/">Section 60</a>, unless the corporate debtor itself is already subject to such pending proceedings before the Tribunal.</p>
<p title="Analysis of the law" data-structure="Section">In the psent case, as already noticed (in paragraph 23 (iii) of this judgment, supra), the application filed by the psent petitioner (copy Annexure P-17), under the provisions of <a href="/doc/992144/">Section 94(1)</a> of the Code read with Rule 6 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy (Application to Adjudicating Authority for Insolvency Resolution Process for personal Guarantors to corporate Debtors) Rules, 2019 (hereinafter referred to as the Rules of 2019), is to initiate "an insolvency resolution process in respect of VIJAY KUMAR GHAI", which would only be possible, on a bare reading of <a href="/doc/146441/">Section 60 (2)</a>, if the company of which he is a Director and stands as a personal guarantor to, i.e. M/s Priknit Retails Ltd., is already in proceedings before the NCLT for insolvency resolution/liquidation, either initiated by itself or initiated by the two banks or the company as have been made respondents by the petitioner in his application, i.e. M/s ICICI Bank, State Bank of India and ASREC (India) 40 of 48 ::: Downloaded on - 09-07-2022 21:04:17 ::: CRM-M-22685-2021 (O&amp;M) -41- Ltd.</p>
<p title="Petitioner's Argument" data-structure="PetArg">It is not denied that in fact proceedings under <a href="/doc/1674475/">Section 7</a> of the Code were initiated by the State Bank of India against the petitioners' company, i.e. M/s Priknit Retails Ltd., upon which an order was initially passed on 11.09.2019 by the learned Tribunal, after which IA no.138 of 2020 was filed by the Resolution Professional appointed by that forum, under the provisions of <a href="/doc/1096364/">Sections 23(1)</a> and <a href="/doc/1385350/">34</a> of the Code, seeking that the corporate debtor (Priknit) be liquidated as per the procedure <a href="/doc/30816281/">laid down in</a> the Code, with that application having been allowed and with the Resolution Professional himself having been appointed as the Liquidator on 18.05.2020.</p>
<p title="Conclusion" data-structure="Conclusion">Subsequently, Mr. Jagga also produced an order dated 11.05.2022, (after the matter had been put up for rehearing by this court), also passed by the Tribunal, taking on record the progress report in the liquidation proceedings and with the next date of hearing in those proceedings before the Tribunal now being 19.07.2022.</p>
<p title="Conclusion" data-structure="Conclusion">Hence, as regards the basic maintainability of the application of the petitioner in view of the already pending proceedings initiated against the corporate debtor, the application would be maintainable (though of course with no comment made by this court as to whether the application under <a href="/doc/992144/">Section 94 (1)</a> should be accepted on merits or rejected, by the Tribunal).</p>
<p title="Conclusion" data-structure="Conclusion">34. Consequently, the two questions now before this court are:-</p>
<p title="Conclusion">(1) Whether in such circumstances the complaint under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> of the Act of 1881 would also fall within the ambit of the phrases "all the debts" and "any legal actions or proceedings pending in respect of any debt" as occur in clauses (a) and (b)(i) of sub-section (1) respectively of 41 of 48 ::: Downloaded on - 09-07-2022 21:04:17 ::: CRM-M-22685-2021 (O&amp;M) -42-</p>
<p title="Conclusion" data-structure="Conclusion"><a href="/doc/63256/">Section 96</a>, or would the aforesaid expssions be limited to any debt as is concerned or linked in any manner to the corporate debtor for whom the petitioner stands as a personal guarantor, with the respondent herein not being in any manner concerned with the debt of either the corporate debtor or the personal guarantee furnished by the petitioner in respect of the corporate debtor;</p>
<p title="Petitioner's Argument" data-structure="PetArg">(2) If the answer to the aforesaid question is in the affirmative, whether proceedings under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> of the Act would be deemed to have been stayed in terms of <a href="/doc/63256/">Section 96</a> of the Code in view of the fact that the complaint against the petitioner was filed 8 to 9 years prior to the petitioners' application under <a href="/doc/992144/">Section 94</a> and even about 6 years before the initiation of proceedings against the corporate debtor by the State Bank of India under <a href="/doc/1674475/">Section 7</a> of the Code.</p>
<p title="Conclusion" data-structure="Conclusion">35. As regards the first question, there are two ways of interpting the phrases "all the debts" and "any legal actions or proceedings pending in respect of any debt" as are referred to in <a href="/doc/63256/">Section 96</a> of the Code.</p>
<p title="Analysis of the law" data-structure="Section">First, that as per a plain reading of the aforesaid phrases in the provision, once a personal guarantor to a corporate debtor has filed an application under <a href="/doc/992144/">Section 94(1)</a> before the Adjudicating Authority, all legal proceedings in respect of any debt that the personal guarantor is facing, would be covered by the interim moratorium and consequently the proceedings in the complaint filed by the respondent herein under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> of the Act also would remain stayed, such proceedings being in respect of a debt alleged to have been incurred by the petitioner qua the respondent, (with such interim moratorium to continue till the application under <a href="/doc/992144/">Section 94</a> is either rejected or accepted by the Adjudicating Authority. If the application is admitted, proceedings under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> would remain stayed till the proceedings before 42 of 48 ::: Downloaded on - 09-07-2022 21:04:17 ::: CRM-M-22685-2021 (O&amp;M) -43- the Tribunal are taken to their logical conclusion, in terms of <a href="/doc/1525572/">Sections 100</a> and <a href="/doc/980766/">101</a> of the Code).</p>
<p title="Respondent's Argument" data-structure="RespArg">The other interptation that can be given is that the phrases "all legal proceedings" and "any debt", only pertain to debts as are relatable to the corporate debtor in any manner; and any other personal debt incurred by the guarantor to a corporate debtor, as has nothing to do with such corporate debtor or corporate debt, would not be affected in any manner by the application filed under <a href="/doc/992144/">Section 94</a> by the personal guarantor to a corporate debtor and consequently the complaint filed by the respondent herein under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> of the Act can continue wholly independently of the proceedings before the Adjudicating Authority/NCLT.</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">36. To further try and understand as to which of the aforesaid two interptations would apply, the following part of the judgment of the Supme Court (in paragraph 26.1) of V. Ramakrishnans' case (supra) would need to be looked at again:-</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis">".......... and it is clear that in the vast majority of cases, personal guarantees are given by Directors who are in management of the companies. The object of the Code is not to allow such guarantors to escape from an independent and co-extensive liability to pay off the entire outstanding debt, which is why <a href="/doc/173961/">Section 14</a> is not applied to them. However, insofar as firms and individuals are concerned, guarantees are given in respect of individual debts by persons who have unlimited liability to pay them. And such guarantors may be complete strangers to the debtor -- often it could be a personal friend. It is for this reason that the moratorium mentioned in <a href="/doc/980766/">Section 101</a> would cover such persons, as such moratorium is in relation to the debt and not the debtor."</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">43 of 48 ::: Downloaded on - 09-07-2022 21:04:17 ::: CRM-M-22685-2021 (O&amp;M) -44- Further, the judgment in <a href="/doc/30816281/">Lalit Kumar Jains</a>' case (supra) may also be again referred to wherein, while upholding the distinction created between other individuals and personal guarantors to corporate debtors vide sub-section (2) of <a href="/doc/146441/">Section 60</a> of the Code (as regards the forum before which a personal guarantor would be required to apply under <a href="/doc/992144/">Section 94</a>), it was thereafter held in paragraph 100 (Law Finder edition = para 113 SCC edition) as follows:-</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis">"100. It is clear from the above analysis that Parliamentary intent was to treat personal guarantors differently from other categories of individuals. The intimate connection between such individuals and corporate entities to whom they stood guarantee, as well as the possibility of two separate processes being carried on in different forums, with its attendant uncertain outcomes, led to carving out personal guarantors as a separate species of individuals, for whom the Adjudicating Authority was common with the corporate debtor to whom they had stood guarantee. The fact that the process of insolvency in Part III is to be applied to individuals, whereas the process in relation to corporate debtors, set out in Part II is to be applied to such corporate persons, does not lead to incongruity. On the other hand, there appear to be sound reasons why the forum for adjudicating insolvency processes - the provisions of which are disparate- is to be common, i.e. through the NCLT. As was emphasized during the hearing, the NCLT would be able to consider the whole picture, as it were, about the nature of the assets available, either during the corporate debtor's insolvency process, or even later; this would facilitate the CoC in framing realistic plans, keeping in mind the prospect of realizing some part of the creditors' dues from personal guarantors."</p>
<p title="Conclusion" data-structure="Conclusion">(Emphasis applied in this judgment only).</p>
<p title="Analysis of the law" data-structure="Section">37. Hence, it is obviously clear from a reading of the aforesaid part of <a href="/doc/30816281/">the said judgment</a> as also from the relevant provisions of the Code as have 44 of 48 ::: Downloaded on - 09-07-2022 21:04:17 ::: CRM-M-22685-2021 (O&amp;M) -45- been reproduced hereinabove, that personal guarantors to corporate debtors are to be treated differently from other categories of individuals who would be covered by Part III of the Code, with it to be again observed that personal guarantors have however only been defined in <a href="/doc/6856/">Section 5(22)</a> falling in Part II thereof and not in Part III.</p>
<p title="Analysis of the law" data-structure="Section">Yet, the rule making authority under Section 239 of the Code (the Central Government) promulgated the Rules of 2019 by invoking jurisdiction under the said provision as also under the other provisions referred to in the pamble to the rules, and stipulated in Rule 6 therein that an application to be made by such a guarantor under the provisions of <a href="/doc/992144/">Section 94(1)</a> would be submitted in terms of the procedure laid down under that Rule.</p>
<p title="Analysis of the law" data-structure="Section">Thus, the application to be made by a personal guarantor to a corporate debtor, even though such a person/individual is referred to in <a href="/doc/6856/">Section 5(22)</a> and <a href="/doc/146441/">Section 60</a>, both falling in Part II of the Code and not in Part III thereof, is to be made under <a href="/doc/992144/">Section 94(1)</a> falling within Part III and with the said application to be made before the NCLT, in terms of <a href="/doc/146441/">Section 60(1)</a> which falls under Part II of the Code.</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">Now in the aforesaid background, if one is to consider Mr. Jaggas' argument that the petitioner having sought his own insolvency under <a href="/doc/992144/">Section 94</a>, all his debts would necessarily have to be considered by the Tribunal, that would seem to be in consonance with what has been observed in paragraph 100 of <a href="/doc/30816281/">Lalit Kumar Jains</a>' case (reproduced earlier also, supra), to the effect that:-</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis">"As was emphasized during the hearing, the NCLT would be able to consider the whole picture, as it were, about the nature of the assets available, either during the corporate debtor's insolvency 45 of 48 ::: Downloaded on - 09-07-2022 21:04:17 ::: CRM-M-22685-2021 (O&amp;M) -46- process, or even later; this would facilitate the CoC in framing realistic plans, keeping in mind the prospect of realizing some part of the creditors' dues from personal guarantors."</p>
<p title="Conclusion" data-structure="Conclusion">(Emphasis applied in this judgment only).</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">38. Hence, though in the opinion of this court otherwise a proceeding under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> of the Act, qua a debt as is wholly incurred qua an individual who is not in any manner connected to the corporate debtor that the petitioner stood a personal guarantor for, nor to the corporate debt itself, would need to proceed independently so as not to make the complainant in such proceedings under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> suffer further delays, especially when in the psent case he has already suffered a delay of about 10 years since his complaint was initially filed, however, in the light of the aforesaid observations as also the fact that <a href="/doc/63256/">Section 96</a> of the Code does not specifically carve out any exception qua such a debt as is subject matter of an instrument in the context of which a complaint under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> of the Act has been filed, this court would have to interpt the terms "all the debts" and "any legal action or proceedings pending in respect of any debt" as occur in <a href="/doc/63256/">Section 96</a> of the Code, to mean that it would cover all such debts including any debt not pertaining to a corporate debtor for whom the accused in such a complaint under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> stood as a personal guarantor to, even in his capacity as a Director of such corporate debtor.</p>
<p title="Respondent's Argument" data-structure="RespArg">This would be further so in the opinion of this court, because a "debt" has been defined in the absolutely generic meaning of the word, in <a href="/doc/1485731/">Section 3 (11)</a> of the Code (falling in the pliminary Part-I thereof); and further, as admitted by learned counsel for the respondent, a debt as is subject matter of proceedings under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> of the Act, has not been pscribed to 46 of 48 ::: Downloaded on - 09-07-2022 21:04:17 ::: CRM-M-22685-2021 (O&amp;M) -47- be an "excluded debt" in terms of <a href="/doc/621673/">Section 79(e)</a> of the Code.</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">In this regard, it also needs to be observed here that unless the wordings of a statute are "unworkable" or wholly impractical, nothing extra can be read into a statute or taken away therefrom.</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">39. As regards the second question posed to itself by this court in paragraph 34 (supra), it would have to be held that by virtue of the term "any legal action or proceedings pending in respect of any debt (as per <a href="/doc/63256/">Section 96</a>), proceedings under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> of the Act, would be deemed to be stayed irrespective of the fact that such proceedings were initiated far before the application under <a href="/doc/992144/">Section 94</a> of the Code was filed by the personal guarantor to a corporate debtor.</p>
<p title="Precedent Analysis" data-structure="Precedent">In that very context, as regards the dismissal by the Supme Court of other appeals and writ petitions as were heard with P. Mohanrajs' case (as have been pointed to by Mr. Mehta, learned counsel for the respondent), the dismissal would seem to be on account of the fact that the proceedings under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> against the Dire7ctors of the companies as were corporate debtors in those cases, were firstly held to be independent of the proceedings under the Code against the corporate debtor itself and further, there is no interim moratorium referred to in <a href="/doc/173961/">Section 14</a>, with the moratorium mentioned in that provision, being one as has to be declared by the Adjudicating Authority; and consequently the Supme Court held that such declaration having come at a stage far after the proceedings were initiated under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> of the Act, the moratorium would not apply (obviously also because the Directors were treated different to the corporate debtor itself); which is a wholly different situation to that as is postulated in <a href="/doc/63256/">Section 96</a>, <a href="/doc/1821721/">47</a> of 48 ::: Downloaded on - 09-07-2022 21:04:17 ::: CRM-M-22685-2021 (O&amp;M) -48- wherein it is an interim moratorium that comes into effect, by which all proceedings qua any debt of the individual/partnership firm etc. would be deemed to have been stayed.</p>
<p title="Conclusion" data-structure="Conclusion">40. Consequently, even though the respondent herein may suffer longer delays due to the stay that would be deemed to be operating on the proceedings in the complaint filed by him under <a href="/doc/1823824/">Section 138</a> of the Act, by virtue of the interim moratorium stipulated in <a href="/doc/63256/">Section 96</a> of the Code, there would seem to be no option with this court but to allow the petition and set aside the impugned order passed by the learned JMIC, Jalandhar, dated 25.05.2021. It is therefore ordered accordingly.</p>
