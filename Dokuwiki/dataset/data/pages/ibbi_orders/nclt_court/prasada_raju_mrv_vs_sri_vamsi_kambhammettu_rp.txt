	<h1>
		Whether a Suspended Director/Promoter of Corporate Debtor who is also a Resolution Applicant (a dual role) is entitled to access Resolution Plans submitted by others Resolution Applicants, as per Vijay Kumar Jain Vs. Standard Chartered Bank judgment &ndash; Prasada Raju M.R.V Vs. Sri. Vamsi Kambhammettu, RP of Mantena Laboratories Ltd. &ndash; NCLT Hyderabad Bench
	</h1>
	<div>
		February 20, 2024
	</div>
	<p>
		In this important judgment, Hon'ble NCLT Hyderabad Bench holds that: (i) A Resolution Applicant can attend CoC meeting only in respect of the plan that was submitted by him, and will have no access to the Resolution Plans submitted by others. (ii) The cited caselaw of Vijay Kumar Jain Vs. Standard Chartered Bank (2019) 24 SC also does not support the Applicant&rsquo;s argument. (iii) In the present case, not only the Applicant is related to the CD, being a promoter/director, he is also a Resolution Applicant. The CD being the MSME, he was permitted to submit a Resolution Plan. However, he was rightly kept out by the CoC from the proceedings of examination and evaluation of the Resolution Plans submitted by other Resolution Applicants. (iv) The &lsquo;conflict of interest&rsquo; for the Applicant, who was part of the CoC in the dual capacity did not end with him being rejected as Resolution Applicant. His capacity to be an impartial observer of the CoC&rsquo;s deliberations was compromised and his views on the Resolution Plan submitted by any other Resolution Applicant would necessarily have a potential of bias even after he was out of the race.
	</p>
</div>

					<p>
						<strong>
							 I.A. No. 78 of 2023 in I.A. No. 1468 of 2022:
						</strong>
					</p>
					<p>
						Through this IA the Applicant, a Director in the suspended Board of M/s. Mantena Laboratories Limited, the Corporate Debtor, essentially seeks access to the Resolution Plans submitted by the other Resolution Applicants. To this end, he has relied upon the judgment of
						<em>
							 Vijay Kumar Jain Vs. Standard Chartered Bank&nbsp;(2019) 24 SC
						</em>
						 to claim that as per the decision of Hon&rsquo;ble Supreme Court in this case, he is entitled to the records of all the deliberations of the CoC, including all Resolution Plans considered by it.
					</p>
					<p>
						The Adjudicating Authority holds that:
					</p>
					<ul>
						<li>
							It is a fact that the Applicant has a dual role in this case. Besides being part of the suspended Board, he is also one of the Resolution Applicants. As part of the suspended Board, he was given notices of the CoC meetings as required under p 24(3)(b) of IBC. He was also allowed to attend these meetings, except when the CoC was considering Resolution Plans.
						</li>
						<li>
							However, the role of the Applicant as &lsquo;Resolution Applicant&rsquo; is&nbsp;circumscribed. Under p 30(5) of IBC, &ldquo;
							<em>
								 the resolution applicant
							</em>
							<em>
								 may attend the meeting of the committee of creditors in which
							</em>
							<em>
								 resolution plan of the applicant is considered
							</em>
							 &rdquo;. It clearly follows that&nbsp;a Resolution Applicant can attend CoC meeting only in respect of the&nbsp;plan that was submitted by him, and will have no access to the&nbsp;Resolution Plans submitted by others.
						</li>
						<li>
							The cited caselaw of
							<em>
								 Vijay Kumar Jain Vs. Standard Chartered Bank&nbsp;(2019) 24 SC
							</em>
							 also does not support the Applicant&rsquo;s argument. That case dealt with the&nbsp;eligibility of a former director who, being an unrelated financial creditor of&nbsp;the CD, sought access to CoC meetings and Resolution Plans submitted&nbsp;by Prospective Resolution Applicants. The Apex Court decision in that&nbsp;case affirmed the right of such individuals to attend CoC meetings and&nbsp;access Resolution Plans. However, that judgment will not apply to a&nbsp;suspended promoter/director who is also a Resolution Applicant.
						</li>
						<li>
							In the present case, not only the Applicant is related to the CD, being a promoter/director, he is also a Resolution Applicant. The CD being the MSME, he was permitted to submit a Resolution Plan. However, he was rightly kept out by the CoC from the proceedings of examination and evaluation of the Resolution Plans submitted by other Resolution Applicants.
						</li>
						<li>
							We are also not persuaded by the argument of the Ld Counsel for the Applicant that, after the Applicant&rsquo;s plan was rejected by the CoC the conflict of interest does not survive and he should have been allowed access to all deliberations of the CoC including the Resolution Plans submitted by other Resolution Applicants.
						</li>
						<li>
							The &lsquo;conflict of interest&rsquo; for the Applicant, who was part of the&nbsp;CoC in the dual capacity did not end with him being rejected as&nbsp;Resolution Applicant. His capacity to be an impartial observer of the&nbsp;CoC&rsquo;s deliberations was compromised and his views on the Resolution&nbsp;Plan submitted by any other Resolution Applicant would necessarily&nbsp;have a potential of bias even after he was out of the race.
						</li>
						<li>
							The other argument of the Ld. Counsel that being an expert in the field of&nbsp;pharmaceuticals, the Applicant presents those &lsquo;exceptional&nbsp;circumstances&rsquo; in which it should have not been considered necessary for&nbsp;him to even compete with other Resolution Applicants, is also not&nbsp;convincing. Firstly, in this application, this is not the relief that the&nbsp;Applicant has sought, as he has already competed with other Resolution&nbsp;Applicants. Secondly, it is his &ldquo;expertise&rdquo; in managing the affairs of the&nbsp;CD which has resulted in the present state of affairs. If he was so&nbsp;exceptional, he would not have run a debt of more than Rs 127 crores&nbsp;which the CD was neither able to service nor repay, and was to be&nbsp;admitted into CIRP.About the allegations of the Resolution Applicants being allowed to modify the plan more than once, and thus violative of the Law, is the matter to be examined by this authority while examining IA No. 1468/2023, through which the successful Resolution Plan has been presented. As held earlier, the Applicant has no locus-standi in that application.
						</li>
						<li>
							Applicant being an unsuccessful Resolution Applicant is not a necessary party for the approval of a Resolution Plan. Once the CoC has endorsed a Plan by requisite percentage of voting share, it is only for this Authority to adjudicate thereon and approve it under the provisions of IBC. For the reasons as above, this application is dismissed.
							<strong>
								 (p19-27)
							</strong>
						</li>
					</ul>
					<p>
						<strong>
							 I.A. No. 331 of 2022:
						</strong>
					</p>
					<p>
						This application is filed by the Bank of Maharashtra, one of the Financial Creditors of the Corporate Debtor (CD) M/s. Mantena Laboratories Limited, being aggrieved by the 10th CoC1 meeting held on 24.03.2022, wherein the Applicant did not receive the distribution pattern of the amounts to be received by each Financial Creditor as per the Resolution Plan.
					</p>
					<p>
						The central question raised by the Applicant is whether it is entitled to same percentage of the distribution proceeds of the Resolution Plan as its voting right as a financial creditor.
					</p>
					<p>
						The Adjudicating Authority holds that:
					</p>
					<ul>
						<li>
							What amount is to be&nbsp;paid to different classes or subclasses of creditors in accordance&nbsp;with the provisions of the IBC and the related Regulations, is&nbsp;essentially the commercial wisdom of the Committee of&nbsp;Creditors. In this context, it is to be emphasized that a&nbsp;dissenting secured creditor, such as the Applicant in the present&nbsp;case, cannot dictate the amount to be allocated to it based solely&nbsp;on the value of the security interest.
						</li>
						<li>
							In the present case the Applicant held first charge only over the current assets, value of which was reduced to nil. It could not have sought parity with the other secured creditors who had first charge over the fixed assets and where the Resolution Plan majorly comprised of the value placed on the fixed assets. The priority in which the resolution proceeds were to be distributed to the secured creditors was to be determined by the CoC in its commercial wisdom.
						</li>
						<li>
							With these observations, the prayers of the Applicant are dealt with as below:
							<ul>
								<li>
									(a) As the 9th CoC meeting has already been held and the Resolution Plan approved by CoC with more than the requisite voting share, this prayer has become infructuous.
								</li>
								<li>
									(b) No directions are being given to the CoC for the&nbsp;reasons discussed in the preceding paras.
								</li>
								<li>
									(c) The minutes of 10th CoC meeting have already been provided by the RP to the Applicant. Since the Resolution Plan is still to be approved by this Authority, any determination about share of each of the financial creditor for distribution out of the resolution proceeds is premature.
								</li>
							</ul>
						</li>
						<li>
							The Application is therefore dismissed.
							<strong>
								 (p26-28)
							</strong>
						</li>
					</ul>
