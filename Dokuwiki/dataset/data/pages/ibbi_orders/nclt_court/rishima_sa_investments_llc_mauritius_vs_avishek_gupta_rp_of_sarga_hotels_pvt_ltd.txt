	<h1>
		The debt arising out of foreign arbitral awards cannot be considered as Financial Debt, but the same can be treated as &ldquo;Other Debts&rdquo; and since claim has reached finality in the view of decree of competent foreign arbitral forums and only the execution is pending before High Court, the claim should be admitted in full and provision should have been made out of the Resolution Plan value &ndash; Rishima SA Investments LLC (Mauritius) v. Avishek Gupta, RP of Sarga Hotels Pvt. Ltd. &ndash; NCLT Kolkata Bench
	</h1>
	<div>
		December 5, 2023
	</div>
	<p>
		NCLT Kolkata held that: (i) Since, the Applicant is a &ldquo;decree holder&rdquo; of a foreign award which is already stamped as a decree the same will have to be considered as debt. The debt arising out of these foreign awards cannot be considered as financial debt, but the same can be treated as &ldquo;Other debts&rdquo;. Therefore, the action of the Resolution professional in treating this debt as &ldquo;other debt&rdquo; cannot be faulted. (ii) The claim has reached finality as on date in the view of decree of competent foreign arbitral forums and only the execution is pending before the Hon'ble High Court, Delhi. Considering the prima-facie view of the Delhi High Court as already stated by the Learned Counsel for the Applicant, enforcement can be challenged only on limited grounds mentioned in Section 47 to 49 of Arbitration &amp; Conciliation Act 1996. (iii) The Resolution Professional should have admitted the claim in full and provision should have been made out of the Resolution plan value.
	</p>

					<p>
						<strong>
							 Brief about the decision:
						</strong>
					</p>
					<p>
						<em>
							<strong>
								 Facts of the case
							</strong>
						</em>
					</p>
					<ul>
						<li>
							The Applicant, Rishima SA Investments LLC (Mauritius) acquired 35% shareholding in the corporate debtor by investing Rs. 80 Crore. As per the Share subscription and shareholders agreement (SSHA) made between the parties Shristi Infrastructure Development Corporation Ltd. (SIDCL) and the Corporate Debtor was obliged to purchase Applicant&rsquo;s share holding in corporate debtor at a price equivalent to the amount invested plus a return of 25% per annum as IRR.
						</li>
						<li>
							Since there was a breach of SSHA, the SIDCL and the Corporate debtor failed to&nbsp;purchase the shareholding of the applicant as agreed in the said SSHA, the Applicant initiated dispute for arbitration under the Singapore Arbitration Act 2001 as provided in SSHA, against SIDCL and the corporate debtor.
						</li>
						<li>
							After several rounds of hearing and litigations a final award was made dated 12-07-2020 by the Arbitral Tribunal. By virtue of the foreign arbitral awards SIDCL and the Corporate debtor herein
							<br />
							 are liable to pay Rs. 132,39,33,646/- apart from legal costs amounting to Rs. 50,41,622/- to the Applicant.
						</li>
						<li>
							The said foreign awards have become final in the international arbitration forums.
						</li>
						<li>
							The Applicant has filed petition before the Hon&rsquo;ble High Court, Delhi for enforcement of these awards in terms of Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. The same is pending for disposal.
						</li>
						<li>
							In the meanwhile, CIRP of the Corporate&nbsp;debtor was initiated on 11.02.2022 pursuant to which the Applicant has submitted his claim in Form C as financial debt.
						</li>
						<li>
							The said claim was rejected by the Resolution professional of the Corporate Debtor vide his E-Mail dated 16.03.2022 on the ground that the claim of the Applicant did not meet the criteria of a &ldquo;financial debt&rdquo;, pursuant to which the Applicant filed his claim in Form F dated 21.03.2022 in protest, in the capacity of &ldquo;Other Creditors&rdquo;.
						</li>
						<li>
							The Resolution professional relying upon the observation made by the Hon&rsquo;ble Supreme Court of India in the case of
							<em>
								 Essar Steel Ltd v. Satish
							</em>
							<em>
								 Kumar Gupta and others
							</em>
							 , reported in
							<em>
								 (2019) 07 SC
							</em>
							 , the resolution professional admitted claim of Applicant as contingent claim for notional value of Rs. 1/-.
						</li>
						<li>
							Aggrieved by the action of Resolution professional in admitting the claim of the Applicant as &ldquo;other debts&rdquo; and that too at a notional value of Rs.1 /- the Applicant has filed this application seeking direction to the Resolution professional to allow the claim of INR 132,89,75,268/- made by the Applicant in Form C dated 25.02.2022 as a financial debt and direct the resolution professional to allow the Applicant to attend the meeting of committee of creditors (CoC) in the capacity of financial creditor.
						</li>
					</ul>
					<p>
						<em>
							<strong>
								 Issues
							</strong>
						</em>
					</p>
					<p>
						a. Whether the claim made by the Applicant would qualify as &ldquo;financial debt&rdquo; or &ldquo;other debt&rdquo;.
					</p>
					<p>
						b. Whether the Resolution professional&rsquo;s action of admitting only Rs. 1 against claim of Rs. 132,89,52,568/- is legally correct or not.
					</p>
					<p>
						<em>
							<strong>
								 Decision of the Adjudicating Authority
							</strong>
						</em>
					</p>
					<p>
						<strong>
							 A. Debt arising out of foreign awards cannot be considered as financial debt
						</strong>
					</p>
					<ul>
						<li>
							At no point in time the applicant has given any loan or made any financial accommodation to be called as financial creditor. In other words, none of the ingredients mentioned in Section 5(8) of IBC which deals with &ldquo;financial debt&rdquo; are present in the transactions between the Applicant, SIDCL and Corporate debtor.
							<strong>
								 (p34)
							</strong>
						</li>
						<li>
							Therefore, the Applicant cannot be called as financial creditor, and he is not entitled to participate in the Committee of Creditors (CoC) meeting of the corporate debtor during CIRP process.
							<strong>
								 (p35)
							</strong>
						</li>
						<li>
							Since, the Applicant is a &ldquo;decree holder&rdquo; of a foreign award which is already stamped as a decree the same will have to be considered as debt.
							<strong>
								 The debt arising out of these foreign awards cannot be considered as financial debt, but the same can be treated as &ldquo;Other debts&rdquo;.
							</strong>
							<strong>
								 Therefore, the action of the Resolution professional in treating this debt as &ldquo;other debt&rdquo; cannot be faulted.
								<strong>
									 (p36)
								</strong>
							</strong>
						</li>
					</ul>
					<p>
						<strong>
							 B. Admitting the claim for notional value
						</strong>
					</p>
					<p>
						Now we will deal with the Resolution professional&rsquo;s action of admitting the claim for notional value of only INR 1. In the judgment of
						<em>
							 Essar Steels Ltd Vs. Satish Kumar Gupta and Others&nbsp;(2019) 07 SC
						</em>
						 , the Hon&rsquo;ble Apex Court has noted that the resolution professional admitted the claim of the respondent notionally at INR 1 on the ground that there were several disputes pending before various authorities in respect of said amount.
					</p>
					<ul>
						<li>
							In view of the above the Hon&rsquo;ble Supreme Court held that the resolution professional was correct at only admitting the claim at notional value of INR 1 due to the pendency of disputes with regard to these claims.
							<strong>
								 (p37)
							</strong>
						</li>
						<li>
							Per-contra in the matter in hand, the claim has reached finality as on date in the view of decree of competent foreign arbitral forums and only the execution is pending before the Hon&rsquo;ble High Court, Delhi.&nbsp;Considering the prima-facie view of the Delhi High Court as already stated by the Learned Counsel for the Applicant, enforcement can be challenged only on limited grounds mentioned in Section 47 to 49 of Arbitration &amp; Conciliation Act 1996.
							<strong>
								 (p38-39)
							</strong>
						</li>
						<li>
							When that being the case, the Resolution Professional should have admitted the claim in full and provision should have been made out of the Resolution plan value. On similar facts and circumstances, the Ld. NCLT, Bangaluru Bench has decided the case in the same manner as it has been done in the case in hand. The decision of the Bengaluru Bench is in the case of
							<em>
								 United Spirits Limited v. Mr. Kondisetty Kumar Dushyantha and others
							</em>
							 , being I.A. 307 of 2021 in CP.(IB)No. 147/BB/2018.
							<strong>
								 (p40)
							</strong>
						</li>
						<li>
							However, the plan could state that the value provided against the Applicant&rsquo;s claim in the plan would be paid subject to the outcome of the execution petition pending before the Hon&rsquo;ble Delhi High Court and till such time amounts could be held in escrow account.
							<strong>
								 (p41)
							</strong>
						</li>
						<li>
							In case the Applicant fails in his execution petition, the amount held in the escrow account can be redistributed as per the Commercial wisdom of CoC. The provision in the plan against the claim made by the Applicant may be made based on the commercial wisdom of CoC but to be made on fair and equitable basis keeping in mind, that one of the objectives of IBC is to balance the interests of all the stakeholders.
							<strong>
								 (p42)
							</strong>
						</li>
					</ul>
					<p>
						In view of above this application being I.A. (IB) 1131/KB/2022 filed by Rishima SA Investments LLC (Mauritius), Applicant herein is partly allowed and disposed of in terms of the directions above.
						<strong>
							 (p43)
						</strong>
					</p>
