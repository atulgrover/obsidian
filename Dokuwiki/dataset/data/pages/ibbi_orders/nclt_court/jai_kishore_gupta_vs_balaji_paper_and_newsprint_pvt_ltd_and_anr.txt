<h4>
	The valuation of Corporate Debtor cannot be challenged by anyone when Resolution Plan has already been approved by NCLT | If CoC disagree with Valuation Report, it would direct for fresh valuation, where CoC agrees, the decision of CoC is final and there is no scope for judicial review &ndash; Jai Kishore Gupta Vs. Balaji Paper and Newsprint Pvt. Ltd. and Anr. &ndash; NCLT Kolkata Bench
</h4>
<h4>
	May 4, 2024
</h4>
<p>
	Hon&rsquo;ble NCLT Kolkata Bench held that: (i) The valuation of the Corporate Debtor cannot be challenged at this stage by anyone when the resolution plan has already been approved by us. (ii) Question of valuation carried out by two independent Registered Valuers duly registered with IBBI cannot be challenged by the Appellants as the same being a question of fact is normally not interfered by Courts. (iii) If the CoC disagree, it would direct for fresh valuation. Where the CoC agrees, the decision of the CoC in this regard is final and there is no scope for judicial review. (iv) It is the duty of the Financial Creditors and the Resolution Professional, to invoke personal guarantees as well as corporate guarantees furnished for the loans and advances taken by the Corporate Debtor for the purpose of maximisation of wealth of the Corporate Debtor.
</p>

	<hr />
	<p>
		<strong>
			 Brief about the decision:
		</strong>
	</p>
	<p>
		<em>
			<strong>
				 Background
			</strong>
		</em>
	</p>
	<p>
		This application has been filed by one Jai Kishore Gupta proprietor of M/s. Lalta Prasad Shaw &amp; Co. for recalling of an Order dated 12.01.2024 wherein the NCLT approved the Resolution Plan presented before it relating to Resolution Plan of the Corporate Debtor Balaji Paper and News Print Pvt. Ltd.
	</p>
	<p>
		In this application three challenges have been made to the approved resolution plan which are as under: &ndash;
	</p>
	<ul>
		<li>
			(i) The Corporate Debtor has been undervalued deliberately to allocate less value for Operational
			<br />
			 Creditors.
		</li>
		<li>
			(ii) To personal and corporate guarantees given to bankers (&ldquo;FC&rdquo;) have not been invoked resulting in lesser maximisation of wealth of the Corporate Debtor which led to highly disproportionate allotment of a plan value of the Operational Creditors.
		</li>
		<li>
			(iii) The Personal Guarantors&rsquo; and the Corporate Debtors&rsquo; have transferred their assets to others immediately after the commencement of CIRP against which no action has been taken by the Resolution Professional / Financial Creditors&rsquo; resulting in grave injustice to Operational Creditor allocation in terms of funds. The applicant has alleged collusion between Financial Creditors, Corporate Debtors, Suspended Board and Resolution Professional in order to allocate only 0.23% of the admitted claim of the Operational Creditors&rsquo;, whereas the Financial Creditors through CoC allotted to themselves nearly 89% of the plan value.
		</li>
	</ul>
	<p>
		<em>
			<strong>
				 Decision of Adjudicating Authority
			</strong>
		</em>
	</p>
	<p>
		<strong>
			 A. Valuation
		</strong>
	</p>
	<ul>
		<li>
			(i)
			<strong>
				 The valuation of the Corporate Debtor cannot be
			</strong>
			<strong>
				 challenged at this stage by anyone when the resolution
			</strong>
			<strong>
				 plan has already been approved by us.
			</strong>
		</li>
		<li>
			(ii) It is settled law that as long as the valuer so appointed, has applied an internationally accepted standard and has basis for applying a particular standard over another, then, the same is a &ldquo;professional&rdquo; judgment of such a valuer and the CoC, in its &ldquo;commercial wisdom&rdquo; may agree or disagree with such a valuation. Such decision of the CoC cannot be challenged. This position of law is noted by the Hon&rsquo;ble NCLAT in
			<em>
				 Mandip Singh v. Mahesh Bansal (Liquidator)
			</em>
			 reported at
			<em>
				 (2022) ibclaw.in 511 NCLAT
			</em>
			 , wherein the Appellate Authority held that it is settled position of law that
			<strong>
				 question of valuation carried out by two independent Registered Valuers duly registered with IBBI cannot be challenged by the Appellants as the same being a question of fact is normally not interfered by Courts.
			</strong>
		</li>
		<li>
			(iii)
			<strong>
				 If the CoC disagree, it would direct for fresh valuation. Where the CoC agrees, the decision of the CoC in this regard is final and there is no scope for judicial review.
			</strong>
		</li>
		<li>
			(iv) Also, in the present case, the applicant has not provided any basis for challenge to the valuation. In view of above, we reject this allegation as legally and factually not tenable.
			<strong>
				 (p7.2)
			</strong>
		</li>
	</ul>
	<p>
		<strong>
			 B. Personal Guarantees and Corporate Guarantees
		</strong>
	</p>
	<ul>
		<li>
			(i) As on date no application has been preferred by Financial Creditors under Section 94 and 95 of Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code.
		</li>
		<li>
			(ii)
			<strong>
				 It is the duty of the Financial Creditors and the Resolution Professional, to invoke personal guarantees as well as corporate guarantees furnished for the loans and advances taken by the Corporate Debtor for the purpose of maximisation of wealth of the Corporate Debtor.
			</strong>
		</li>
		<li>
			(iv) If personal guarantees given by the suspended board and corporate guarantees given by the corporates had been invoked and applications under Sections 94 and 95 of IB, Code are pursued to initiate insolvency resolution process of such guarantors that would have resulted in maximisation of wealth of the corporate debtor, which is one of the key objectives of IB, Code. Such an act would have led to more money available for distribution. In such of an event, the balance of interest of all stakeholders including the&nbsp;Operational Creditors in this case could have been achieved.
			<strong>
				 (p7.3)
			</strong>
		</li>
	</ul>
	<p>
		<strong>
			 C.
		</strong>
		<strong>
			 Transfer of assets of Personal Guarantors to others
		</strong>
	</p>
	<ul>
		<li>
			(i) Transfer of assets by the personal guarantors and the corporate guarantors immediately, after the commencement of the CIRP, has been alleged. This is without any basis as the applicant has not produced any evidence whatsoever on such alleged transfers made by the Personal Guarantors and Corporate Guarantors immediately after commencement of the CIRP of the Corporate Debtor.
		</li>
		<li>
			(ii) Therefore, without proper basis and supporting for such alleged illegal transfers, this application on that ground is not maintainable.
		</li>
		<li>
			(iii) In view of above, we find no reason for recalling the Order dated 12.01.2024 passed by this Tribunal and, consequently, the relief(s) sought in (a) to (e) cannot be granted.
		</li>
		<li>
			(iv) However, we direct the resolution professional/CoC consisting of Financial Creditors to file an application under Section 94 in respect of corporate guarantees and under&nbsp;Section 95 in respect of personal guarantees&nbsp;within a period of 30&nbsp;days from the date of receipt of this Order.
		</li>
	</ul>
	<p>
		<strong>
			 D. Other
		</strong>
	</p>
	<ul>
		<li>
			(v) We have also noted that applications and avoidance transactions have been filed, though the resolution plan states that no application for avoidance transactions have been filed till date against the Corporate Debtor, at the time of filing the application for approval of resolution plan.
		</li>
		<li>
			(vi) However, it is contended in point 4.5 at page 16 of the Resolution Plan as approved by the CoC that the surplus cash and amounts as maybe recovered from the ex-promoters pursuant to the NCLT Orders on the preferential transaction/Avoidance application of the Resolution Professional shall be available to the CoC and any recovery such transaction by the order of the NCLT shall accrue to Financial Creditors in proposition to the amount as proposed in the Resolution Plan. It is further clarified that any application pending on respect of such transaction maybe continued by the Financial Creditors or as may be decided by the Committee of&nbsp;Creditors and any legal expenditure or the associated&nbsp;cost related to PUFF application, shall be borne by the&nbsp;said financial creditors or as may be decided by the&nbsp;CoC.
		</li>
		<li>
			(vii) In this case, the admitted claim of the Operational Creditor is Rs. 43,84,80,083/- which is nearly 43 per cent of the total debt, out of which only 0.23 per cent has been allocated. The reasons for such a massive haircut, particularly to Operational Creditors would need thorough examination and we would expect RP to examine and filed appropriate avoidance applications, if not filed already, to recover any money transferred illegally for the purpose of maximisation of wealth of the Corporate Debtor. Upon realisation of amount pursuant to PUFE application allocations, of the same maybe done by the CoC. It goes without saying that if the repayment plan to be provided on application under Section 94 &amp; 95 of IBC, 2016 is sufficient to meet the balance 12 % debt of Financial Creditor then the entire recovery from PUFE applications may be allocated to Operational Creditor in proportion to the value of debt owed to them treating Operational Creditors as a &ldquo;Separate Class&rdquo; for the purpose of distribution, after deducting cost of such PUFE application incurred. This direction is issued in terms&nbsp;of Section 60(5) of IBC, 2016 read with Rule 11 of NCLT&nbsp;Rules in the interest of justice.
			<strong>
				 (p7.4)
			</strong>
		</li>
		<li>
			Accordingly, this application is disposed on above terms.
			<strong>
				 (p8)
			</strong>
		</li>
	</ul>
