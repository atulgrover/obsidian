## **Streamlining India's CIRP: A Study Guide**

**Glossary of Key Terms**

* **CIRP (Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process):** A time-bound process outlined in the IBC for resolving insolvency in companies.  
* **IBC (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016):** The Indian legislation governing insolvency and bankruptcy proceedings for companies and individuals.  
* **NCLT (National Company Law Tribunal):** The adjudicating authority for matters related to the IBC, including initiation and oversight of CIRP.  
* **NCLAT (National Company Law Appellate Tribunal):** The appellate authority for appeals against orders of the NCLT.  
* **CoC (Committee of Creditors):** A committee formed by financial creditors of a company undergoing CIRP, having the authority to approve a resolution plan.  
* **IRP (Interim Resolution Professional):** A licensed professional appointed to manage the company’s affairs during the initial phase of CIRP.  
* **RP (Resolution Professional):** Takes over from the IRP and manages the company, facilitates the resolution process, and acts as the CoC's advisor.  
* **PPIRP (Pre-Packaged Insolvency Resolution Process):** A faster insolvency resolution mechanism where a resolution plan is pre-negotiated before initiating formal proceedings.  
* **Liquidation Value:** The estimated value of a company's assets if they were sold individually in a forced sale.  
* **Fair Value:** The estimated value of a company’s assets based on their market worth and potential future earnings.  
* **Haircut:** The percentage reduction in the value of a debt that a creditor agrees to accept as part of a resolution plan.  
* **PUFE (Preferential, Undervalued, Fraudulent, and Extortionate Transactions):** Transactions undertaken by a company in distress that are deemed unfair to creditors and can be challenged under the IBC.

**Answer Key**

* The main objective of the IBC is to provide a time-bound framework for resolving insolvency in a fair and efficient manner to maximize the value of assets and promote entrepreneurship. It achieves this by establishing a clear process, timelines, and institutions to handle insolvency cases.  
* The IBC has shifted the debtor-creditor relationship from debtor-oriented to creditor-oriented. The fear of losing control of their companies through CIRP incentivizes debtors to settle dues promptly, leading to financial discipline.  
* A recovery-oriented approach focuses solely on maximizing creditor recovery, often through liquidation of assets. A resolution-oriented approach prioritizes reviving the company as a going concern, preserving jobs and maximizing value for all stakeholders.  
* CIRP outcomes are evaluated based on realized value for creditors, often compared to liquidation value and fair value at admission. Comparing solely to admitted claims is inaccurate as these include penal charges and recovery costs, not reflecting true asset value.  
* Challenges faced by CIRP include: a) Adherence to strict timelines is difficult due to litigation and NCLT burden. b) Lack of a robust PPIRP framework for non-MSME companies delays resolution. c) Absence of a strong secondary market for stressed assets hinders efficient resolution.  
* To address admission delays, suggestions include: a) Increasing the number of NCLT benches dedicated to IBC cases. b) Making only two requirements mandatory for admission: compliance with the Limitation Act and NeSL default certificate. c) Utilizing CIC reports as an additional tool for admission when default isn't registered with NeSL.  
* Two proposed solutions: a) Creating a utility for online document upload by stakeholders to expedite claim verification. b) Dispensing with the NCLT approval requirement for changing IRP/RP to speed up the process.  
* Technology can be used to create an integrated IT platform for end-to-end digitization of CIRP processes. This would ensure transparency, minimize delays, increase stakeholder participation, facilitate effective decision-making, and maximize value realization.  
* A dedicated framework for specific sectors is necessary due to their unique features and complexities that may not be adequately addressed by a general insolvency framework. This would allow for more tailored solutions and efficient resolution.  
* India can learn from Singapore's streamlined timelines and skilled insolvency professionals model for efficient case management. The UK’s pre-insolvency rescue options could provide struggling companies a chance to recover before resorting to formal CIRP proceedings.

# **IBC and the CIRP: Frequently Asked Questions**

**1\. What is the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) and how does it impact India's economy?**  
The IBC, enacted in 2016, is a comprehensive legal framework that governs insolvency resolution for companies in India. It aims to provide a time-bound and efficient process for resolving financial distress, known as the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP). The IBC has significantly impacted the Indian economy by:

* **Shifting the debtor-creditor relationship:** It empowers creditors and incentivizes debtors to settle dues promptly to avoid losing control of their companies.  
* **Facilitating resolution over liquidation:** The IBC prioritizes reviving distressed companies as going concerns, preserving jobs and maximizing asset value.  
* **Improving the financial health of banks:** It strengthens the resolution process for stressed assets, leading to improved asset quality and stronger balance sheets for banks.

**2\. How successful has the IBC been in terms of realizing value for creditors?**  
While the recovery rate for creditors compared to their admitted claims is around 32%, the IBC has been successful in realizing 162% of the liquidation value of assets. This indicates that the IBC helps salvage value that would otherwise be lost in liquidation. The IBC has also demonstrably improved the operational performance and market valuation of resolved companies, further validating its effectiveness.  
**3\. What are some challenges faced in the implementation of the CIRP?**  
Despite its successes, the CIRP faces several challenges, including:

* **Delays due to litigation:** Adherence to strict timelines is often hampered by litigation and appeals, burdening the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT).  
* **Lack of a pre-packaged insolvency framework:** The absence of a robust pre-packaged insolvency resolution framework (PPIRP) for non-MSME companies delays resolution for viable businesses.  
* **Inadequate secondary market for stressed assets:** The lack of a vibrant secondary market hinders efficient resolution and value maximization.  
* **Ambiguity in group insolvency:** Clear guidelines for resolving insolvency cases involving interlinked companies are lacking.  
* **Scope for improvement in cross-border insolvency mechanisms:** The existing framework for cross-border insolvency resolution needs further refinement.

**4\. What measures can be taken to streamline the admission process under the IBC?**  
To expedite the admission of cases under the IBC:

* **Increase the number of NCLT benches:** Allocate sufficient resources and establish specialized benches dedicated to IBC cases.  
* **Simplify admission requirements:** Mandate only compliance with the Limitation Act and the NeSL default certificate for admission.  
* **Curb delaying tactics by debtors:** Limit opportunities for debtors to delay admission and define clear reasons for application rejection.  
* **Utilize Credit Information Companies (CIC) reports:** Leverage CIC reports as an additional tool for verifying defaults when NeSL registration is pending.

**5\. How can the resolution process under the CIRP be further streamlined?**  
Several steps can be taken to improve the efficiency of the resolution process, including:

* **Create a centralized document upload portal:** Develop a utility on the IBBI portal for stakeholders to upload supporting documents, speeding up claim verification.  
* **Empower the Committee of Creditors (CoC):** Eliminate the requirement of NCLT approval for changing the IRP/RP to expedite the process.  
* **Ensure timely resolution of interim applications:** Prevent stays on interim applications without CoC representation and consolidate similar applications.  
* **Grant flexibility in determining the look-back period:** Allow the CoC to determine the look-back period for each case with an outer limit to facilitate effective avoidance actions.  
* **Establish special courts for PUFE trials:** Expedite the trial of PUFE applications in dedicated courts to recover diverted funds and punish fraudsters.

**6\. How can technology be leveraged to enhance the effectiveness of the IBC ecosystem?**  
Integrating technology into the IBC ecosystem can significantly improve its efficiency and transparency:

* **Automate judicial infrastructure and processes:** Introduce e-filing for applications, rejoinders, and affidavits to minimize administrative delays.  
* **Utilize the India Investment Grid (IIG) platform:** Encourage RPs to register CIRP cases on the IIG platform to attract potential investors for stressed assets.  
* **Develop a comprehensive IT platform:** Create an integrated platform for end-to-end digitization of IBC processes, ensuring transparency and effective decision-making.

**7\. What are some other suggestions for enhancing the IBC framework?**

* **Develop sector-specific frameworks:** Create dedicated frameworks for resolving insolvency in sectors like financial services, real estate, telecom, power, and infrastructure.  
* **Ensure timely communication with creditors:** Mandate electronic communication of NCLT admission to all financial creditors, especially those registered with MCA.  
* **Strengthen provisions for cross-border and group insolvency:** Enhance legislative provisions to streamline cross-border insolvency resolution and group insolvency cases.  
* **Expand the applicability of the PPIRP:** Extend the PPIRP framework to all companies to facilitate faster resolution and allow eligible promoters to retain control.

**8\. What lessons can India learn from other countries to improve the CIRP?**  
Adopting best practices from countries like Singapore and the UK can further strengthen the CIRP:

* **Streamlined timelines:** Emulate Singapore's approach to expedite the resolution process by adhering to strict timelines.  
* **Pre-insolvency rescue options:** Incorporate pre-insolvency rescue options, similar to the UK's framework, to provide struggling companies with alternatives to formal CIRP.  
* **Developing a pool of skilled professionals:** Invest in training and development of insolvency professionals to ensure efficient case management, mirroring Singapore's model.
