# Assessment of CIRP Timelines

This study, commissioned by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI), analyzes delays in the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) in India. The research focuses on identifying specific stages within the CIRP where delays are most common and explores whether certain sectors experience more delays than others. The study uses both primary and secondary data: analysis of CIRP forms and a survey of insolvency professionals. The findings reveal that delays occur not only at the end of state agencies but also due to market participants. The study then examines the relationship between debt size and delay, finding no clear correlation. Finally, the report proposes recommendations to improve the efficiency of the CIRP process, such as strengthening the capacity of the courts, improving documentation management, addressing non-cooperation by corporate debtors, and developing a more robust market for distressed assets.

## **Briefing Doc: Assessment of Corporate Insolvency and Resolution Timeline in India**

**Source:** "Assessment of Corporate Insolvency and Resolution Timeline" by Neeti Shikha and Urvashi Shahi, IBBI Research Initiative, February 2021\.  
**Main Themes:**

* **Delays in the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP):** The study identifies significant delays in the CIRP process in India, exceeding the prescribed timelines set by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC).  
* **Stage-specific Delays:** Delays are particularly prevalent at the stages of admission of the CIRP application, issuance of Expression of Interest (EOI) and Request for Resolution Plan (RFRP), finalization of Resolution Applicants (RAs), and approval of the resolution plan.  
* **Reasons for Delay:** Key contributors to delays include:  
* Inadequate capacity of the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT).  
* Difficulty in marketing stressed assets.  
* Lack of cooperation from Corporate Debtors (CDs).  
* Poor documentation practices of companies.  
* **Impact of Delay:** Delays lead to value erosion of distressed assets and hinder the effectiveness of the CIRP as a business rescue mechanism.  
* **Recommendations for Improvement:** The study proposes several recommendations to address delays:  
* Enhancing the capacity of NCLT.  
* Strengthening documentation and information management systems.  
* Addressing non-cooperation by CDs.  
* Developing a robust market for stressed assets.

**Key Findings and Facts:**

* **Admission Delays:**"Admission of application for CIRP takes much longer than the prescribed timeline, both in delayed and control group, suggesting inordinate delay from the side of the adjudicating authority in admitting an application for CIRP."  
* 74% of insolvency professionals surveyed believe it takes over 90 days to initiate CIRP, while the legal timeline is 14 days.  
* **Resolution Plan Approval Delays:** 27.4% of the total delay is attributed to obtaining approvals for the resolution plan from the Committee of Creditors (CoC) and NCLT.  
* **Challenges in Marketing Stressed Assets:**"Issuance of EOI is significantly delayed in companies whose resolution has not been completed within the prescribed timeline."  
* The study suggests a scarcity of good resolution plans and limited participation from RAs.  
* **Non-Cooperation by CDs:**"Research indicates that, as part of the CIRP conducted under the Code, the corporate debtor does not fully cooperate with the RP and that is one of the major reasons for delay in the entire CIRP."  
* 79% of surveyed RPs believe there is a general reluctance to share information by CDs.  
* **Documentation Issues:**"83% respondents had stated that the companies lack a proper documentation model for both statutory and non-statutory records."  
* This lack of proper documentation makes it difficult for RPs to access critical information and hinders the CIRP process.  
* **Sector-specific Delays:**The study observes more delays in the service industry compared to the manufacturing industry, potentially due to challenges in valuing intangible assets.  
* **Debt Size and Delay:** The research finds no significant correlation between debt size and delays in the CIRP.  
* **Focus on Recovery over Revival:**"The study thus far has shown that the model timeline as prescribed by IBBI has not been adhered to leading to delay in insolvency resolution process."  
* The study highlights concerns about the CoC's preference for upfront payments and high recovery rates, potentially leading to the rejection of resolution plans that prioritize business revival.

**Important Quotes:**

* "Speed is of essence for the working of the bankruptcy code... Delays cause value destruction."  
* "The findings of the study suggested following as main reasons for delay- 1\. Inadequate capacity of NCLT 2\. Difficulty in marketing stressed assets 3\. Non-Cooperation by CD 4\. Improper documentation model of companies"  
* "In conclusion, it is submitted that the IBC has indeed been a game changer in providing a timely resolution framework. The gap that exists between the letter of law and the practice as seen in this study, can be redeemed if a pragmatic approach is adopted in strengthening the existing insolvency framework as suggested under the project."

**Overall Conclusion:**  
The study provides valuable insights into the operational challenges and delays plaguing the CIRP process in India. While the IBC has been a significant step towards establishing a time-bound insolvency resolution framework, the research highlights the need for addressing critical bottlenecks, particularly at the NCLT, improving information availability, and promoting a more balanced approach towards business rescue and creditor recovery.

## **Timeline of Events in the CIRP**

This timeline outlines the main events of the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) as described in the provided research paper, focusing on the delays experienced at various stages.  
**T Represents the Date of Commencement of CIRP**  
**Pre-Commencement Stage:**

* **T \- 133 days (average):** Admission of Application for CIRP. Significant delays occur at this stage for both Delayed and Control Groups, indicating a bottleneck at the Adjudicating Authority (NCLT).

**Post-Admission Stage:**

* **T \+ 3 days:** Public Announcement inviting claims. Minor delays observed in both groups.  
* **T \+ 22-35 days:** Submission of Claims. Completed before the prescribed timeline of 90 days. Control Group tends to receive claims faster.  
* **T \+ 25 days:** Constitution of the Committee of Creditors (CoC). Completed close to the prescribed timeline.  
* **T \+ 32 days:** First Meeting of the CoC. Held almost on time, with minor delays in both groups.  
* **T \+ 45-60 days:** Resolution to appoint Resolution Professional (RP). Delays witnessed in both groups. Control Group tends to appoint the RP faster.  
* **T \+ 55 days:** Submission of Information Memorandum (IM) to CoC. Completed around the prescribed timeline.  
* **T \+ 71-120 days:** Issue of Expression of Interest (EOI). Significant delays in the Delayed Group due to multiple re-issuances of EOI, likely caused by a lack of suitable Resolution Applicants (RAs).  
* **T \+ 117-130 days:** Issue of Provisional List of RAs. Both groups exceed the prescribed timeline, with more pronounced delays in the Delayed Group.  
* **T \+ 120-166 days:** Issue of Final List of RAs. Control Group experiences minor delays, while Delayed Group faces significant delays.  
* **T \+ 95-108 days:** Issue of Evaluation Matrix. Control Group completes on time, while Delayed Group experiences minor delays.  
* **T \+ 114-151 days:** Issue of Request for Resolution Plan (RFRP). Delayed Group experiences substantial delays.  
* **T \+ 133-138 days:** Submission of Resolution Plan. Most RAs submit within the timeframe. Delayed Group experiences minor delays.  
* **T \+ 214-383 days:** Approval of Resolution Plan. Substantial delays in both groups, with the Delayed Group taking over 300 days. Most extensions and litigation occur at this stage.

**Reasons for Delays:**

* **Inadequate capacity of NCLT:** Slow processing of applications and approvals.  
* **Difficulty in marketing stressed assets:** Lack of a robust market for stressed assets in India, leading to a shortage of viable RAs.  
* **Non-cooperation by Corporate Debtor (CD):** CDs often withhold crucial financial and operational information, hindering the process.  
* **Improper documentation model of companies:** Companies lack proper documentation for both statutory and non-statutory records, further complicating information gathering.

## **Cast of Characters**

**1\. Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI):** The regulatory body overseeing the implementation of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC). Responsible for setting regulations and guidelines, including the model timeline for CIRP.  
**2\. National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT):** The Adjudicating Authority responsible for admitting applications under CIRP and approving resolution plans.  
**3\. Resolution Professional (RP):** An insolvency professional appointed by the CoC to manage the CIRP, including collating claims, issuing EOI/RFRP, and overseeing the resolution process.  
**4\. Interim Resolution Professional (IRP):** An insolvency professional appointed by the NCLT to manage the initial phase of CIRP before the CoC is constituted.  
**5\. Committee of Creditors (CoC):** A committee formed by the financial creditors of the CD, responsible for making key decisions in the CIRP, including approving the resolution plan.  
**6\. Corporate Debtor (CD):** The company undergoing insolvency resolution under CIRP.  
**7\. Resolution Applicant (RA):** An entity that submits a resolution plan to acquire and revive the CD.  
**8\. Financial Creditors:** Entities to whom the CD owes financial debt, typically banks and financial institutions.  
**9\. Operational Creditors:** Entities to whom the CD owes operational debt for goods or services rendered.  
**10\. Neeti Shikha:** Head of the Centre for Insolvency & Bankruptcy at the Indian Institute of Corporate Affairs, and one of the authors of the research paper.  
**11\. Urvashi Shahi:** Senior Research Fellow at the Centre for Insolvency & Bankruptcy at the Indian Institute of Corporate Affairs, and one of the authors of the research paper.

**Glossary of Key Terms**

* **CIRP:** Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process  
* **IBC:** Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016  
* **CD:** Corporate Debtor  
* **RP:** Resolution Professional  
* **IRP:** Interim Resolution Professional  
* **CoC:** Committee of Creditors  
* **NCLT:** National Company Law Tribunal  
* **EOI:** Expression of Interest  
* **RFRP:** Request for Resolution Plan  
* **RA:** Resolution Applicant  
* **IM:** Information Memorandum  
* **Haircut:** The reduction in the value of a debt or asset that a creditor agrees to accept in a resolution plan.  
* **Liquidation Value:** The estimated value of a company's assets if they were sold off individually in a liquidation scenario.  
* **Multi-track Approach:** A judicial system that uses different procedures for handling cases based on factors like complexity, value, or urgency.  
* **Stressed Asset:** An asset that has experienced a significant decline in value due to financial distress or economic factors.  
* **Information Asymmetry:** A situation where one party in a transaction has more or better information than the other party.  
* **Section 19(2):** A provision in the IBC that allows the RP to approach the NCLT to seek necessary orders to ensure the CD's cooperation in the CIRP.  
* **Section 29A:** A provision in the IBC that disqualifies certain individuals or entities from submitting resolution plans, including those with a history of fraud or wilful defaulters.

# **Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) in India: FAQs**

## **1\. What is the CIRP and how long is it supposed to take?**

The Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) is a legally mandated process in India for resolving insolvency in a timely manner. According to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) of 2016, it should ideally be completed within 180 days, with a possible 90-day extension, totaling 270 days. However, in practice, many cases exceed this timeline.

## **2\. What are the most common delays in the CIRP?**

While delays are prevalent at various stages, the research identifies significant delays primarily at these stages:

* **Admission of the application:** NCLT approval often takes much longer than the stipulated 14 days.  
* **Issuance of Expression of Interest (EOI):** Attracting suitable bidders and re-issuing EOIs due to a lack of viable resolution plans contributes to delays.  
* **Issuance of the final list of Resolution Applicants (RAs):** Delays occur due to the evaluation of applicant eligibility and addressing objections.  
* **Approval of the Resolution Plan:** Obtaining approvals from both the Committee of Creditors (CoC) and the Adjudicating Authority often takes longer than the allocated time.

## **3\. Does the size of debt influence the duration of the CIRP?**

While there is a perception that larger debt leads to longer CIRP, the research found no statistically significant correlation between the debt size and the time taken for resolution.

## **4\. Are delays specific to certain industry sectors?**

The study did not find a strong correlation between delays and specific sectors. However, companies in the service industry generally experience longer delays compared to manufacturing companies, possibly due to difficulties in valuing intangible assets.

## **5\. What are some factors contributing to delays in the CIRP?**

Several key factors have been identified:

* **Inadequate capacity of the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT):** A backlog of cases and limited resources contribute to delays in admissions and resolution plan approvals.  
* **Difficulty in marketing stressed assets:** Attracting potential buyers for stressed assets and developing a robust market for such assets remains a challenge.  
* **Non-cooperation by the Corporate Debtor (CD):** Delays occur when CDs withhold information or fail to cooperate with the Resolution Professional (RP).  
* **Improper documentation practices:** Lack of proper documentation within companies hinders the insolvency resolution process.

## **6\. What measures can be taken to improve the efficiency of CIRP?**

The research proposes several recommendations:

* **Strengthening the capacity of the NCLT:** This involves increasing resources, implementing multi-track approaches for cases with varying complexities, and training judges for greater expertise in insolvency matters.  
* **Improving documentation practices:** Encouraging electronic record keeping and better utilization of Information Utilities can enhance transparency and streamline information access.  
* **Addressing Non-Cooperation by CDs:** Amending legal provisions to penalize non-cooperation can deter CDs from withholding information.  
* **Developing a robust market for stressed assets:** Creating an e-platform for transparent trading of stressed assets, similar to investindia.gov.in, can attract more investors and facilitate efficient price discovery.

## **7\. What are some observations regarding CoC behavior in the CIRP?**

The research notes that the CoC often prioritizes upfront payments and low haircuts, sometimes rejecting resolution plans that offer lower recovery rates but better chances of business revival. This suggests a potential bias towards liquidation over the revival of distressed companies.

## **8\. How effective has the IBC been in resolving insolvency cases?**

Despite the delays, the IBC has brought significant improvements to the insolvency resolution framework in India. The average resolution time has decreased since the IBC's implementation, but there is still a need for improvement to meet global best practices. Continuous efforts to address the identified challenges are crucial to ensure that the Code remains effective in resolving insolvencies and facilitating economic growth.
