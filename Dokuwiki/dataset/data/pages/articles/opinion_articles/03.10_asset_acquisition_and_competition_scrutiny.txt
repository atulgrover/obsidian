# Asset Acquisition and Competition Analysis

This research paper examines the proposal to automatically approve Insolvency Resolution Plans (IRPs) that meet merger thresholds set by the Competition Commission of India (CCI), a practice known as "green channelling." The paper argues that green channelling IRPs would be problematic as the failing firm defense, which underpins the proposal, does not adequately address the potential for anticompetitive effects. The authors analyze the CCI's decisions on IRPs and find that none of them consider the objectives or provisions of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code. They also compare the Indian regime to the EU's merger control regime, highlighting the importance of a thorough competition assessment, especially in the context of distressed firms. The paper concludes that green channelling IRPs is not advisable, and recommends that the CCI conduct a comprehensive review of IRPs that meet merger thresholds to ensure a balanced approach that considers both the objectives of insolvency law and competition law.

### **II. Merger Control in the EU**

**A. Background** This section explores the intricacies of the European Union’s (EU) approach to mergers and acquisitions within its single market framework, highlighting the need for firms to consider the impacts of their actions across all member states. It also introduces the role of the European Commission (EC) as the primary authority responsible for enforcing EU competition law.  
**B. EU Merger Regulation** This subsection delves into the pre-merger notification regime established by the EU Merger Regulation (EUMR), which requires mergers exceeding certain thresholds (similar to India's combination thresholds) to be notified to the EC for scrutiny before implementation. It also examines the ‘suspensory’ nature of the EUMR, effectively putting mergers on hold until cleared by the EC.  
**C. Assessment of Mergers under the EUMR** This section explores the criteria employed by the EC when assessing the potential anti-competitive effects of a proposed merger. It delves into concepts such as ‘Significant Impediment to Effective Competition (SIEC),’ dominance, market power, and the importance of countervailing factors like buyer power and barriers to entry in determining the overall impact on competition.

### **III. The Failing Firm Defence**

**A. Rationale and Application** This section introduces the concept of the ‘failing firm defence,’ an exception within merger control that allows otherwise anti-competitive mergers to be approved if the target firm is demonstrably failing. It highlights how this defence aims to minimize the detrimental effects on competition by considering the potential market consequences if the target firm were allowed to exit the market.  
**B. Three Tests for Failing Firm Defence** This subsection outlines the three key tests employed by the EC to evaluate the validity of a failing firm defence claim:

* Evidence of the firm's imminent failure without the merger.  
* Lack of less anti-competitive alternative acquisition options.  
* Demonstration that the target firm’s assets would exit the market if the merger is blocked.

**C. Case Law Analysis** This section analyzes several landmark EC decisions regarding the failing firm defence, showcasing its application and evolution through cases like *Kali und Salz, Saint Gobain, Blokker/Toys R Us,* and *BASF/Euridol/Pantochim*. It highlights the varying degrees of scrutiny applied by the EC and the factors considered when evaluating the three tests.  
**D. The Failing Division Defence** This subsection discusses the related concept of ‘failing division defence,’ where the argument for the merger rests on the imminent failure of a specific division within a larger company. It explores the challenges and limitations of this defence, emphasizing the need for robust evidence to prevent its misuse for circumventing competition scrutiny. The section utilizes the EC's analysis of the *Aerospatiale-Alenia/De Havilland* merger as a case study to illustrate these challenges.

### **IV. Merger Control in India**

**A. Overview of the Competition Act, 2002** This section provides a concise overview of India's Competition Act, 2002, highlighting its objective to prevent practices having an appreciable adverse effect on competition (AAEC). It explains the concept of ‘combinations’ (mergers and acquisitions exceeding specified thresholds) and the pre-merger notification requirement for such transactions.  
**B. Assessment of Combinations by the CCI** This subsection elaborates on the process followed by the Competition Commission of India (CCI) when evaluating combinations. It discusses the various factors considered by the CCI to determine the potential AAEC of a merger, including market share, concentration levels, barriers to entry, and potential efficiencies gained from the merger.  
**C. The 2019 Amendment and Green Channeling** This section examines the significant changes introduced by the 2019 amendment to the Combination Regulations, particularly the provision for ‘green channeling’ certain categories of combinations that pose no horizontal or vertical overlaps. It explains the rationale behind this expedited approval process and its potential implications for IRPs.

### **V. The UK's Competition and Markets Authority: A Voluntary Merger Regime**

**A. The Enterprise Act of 2002** This section presents an overview of the UK's Enterprise Act of 2002, which governs merger control and introduces a voluntary merger regime. It contrasts this approach with India's pre-merger notification system, highlighting the key differences and implications for businesses.  
**B. Jurisdiction and Procedure of the CMA** This subsection explains the jurisdictional thresholds for merger review by the UK's Competition and Markets Authority (CMA), focusing on factors like turnover and market share. It outlines the procedural stages involved in the CMA's merger assessment process, including initial review, potential in-depth investigation, and possible remedies.  
**C. The Concept of Substantial Lessening of Competition (SLC)** This section delves into the core principle of 'substantial lessening of competition' (SLC) used by the CMA to evaluate mergers. It clarifies that SLC is determined by analyzing the potential reduction in rivalry between firms post-merger, which may manifest in reduced price competitiveness, diminished product quality, or suppressed innovation.  
**D. Theories of Harm** This subsection examines the different ‘theories of harm’ utilized by the CMA to conceptualize and assess the potential negative impacts of mergers on competition. It explores unilateral effects (increased market power), coordinated effects (collusion and collective dominance), and vertical foreclosure (restricting access to upstream or downstream markets).  
**E. The Counterfactual Test** This section explains the CMA's use of a 'counterfactual test' to analyze mergers. This test involves comparing the likely future market state with the merger to a hypothetical scenario without the merger. This method helps determine whether the merger is the primary driver of any predicted SLC.

**C. Comparison of IRP and Regular Combinations** This section compares the CCI's approach to scrutinizing IRPs with its assessment of regular (non-IRP) combinations, particularly within the steel industry (the sector with the most IRP cases). It highlights the similarities and differences in the analytical methods employed, noting the limited use of tools like the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) in IRP cases.  
**D. Limitations of ‘Creditors’ Wisdom’** This subsection critically analyzes the potential limitations of relying solely on the ‘commercial wisdom’ of the Committee of Creditors (CoC) in approving IRPs, especially when competition concerns arise. It examines cases like *Binani Cements* and *Bhushan Power and Steel*, where maximizing creditor recovery might conflict with ensuring a competitive market.  
**E. The Role of the NCLAT** This section proposes leveraging the expertise of the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) to better reconcile competition and insolvency objectives in IRP assessments. It argues that granting the NCLAT original jurisdiction over IRPs meeting combination thresholds would allow for a more holistic review, drawing on expertise in both insolvency and competition law.

It highlights the benefits of this approach in safeguarding consumer interests and ensuring a thorough competition review before mergers are finalized.  
**B. Suitability of Voluntary Merger Regimes** This subsection discusses the applicability of voluntary merger regimes, such as the one in the UK, to IRPs. ### **Answer Key**

* The three tests are: (i) the acquired firm is likely to fail, (ii) no less anti-competitive acquirer exists, and (iii) the firm's assets would exit the market without the merger.  
* The theoretical basis is the "failing firm defense," which permits mergers if the target firm's exit would harm competition more than the merger itself.  
* A suspensory regime requires pre-merger clearance, effectively halting the merger until approval, like India's system. A voluntary regime allows mergers to proceed but can be reviewed later, like the UK's system.  
* Combinations are mergers and acquisitions exceeding specific asset and turnover thresholds, requiring notification to the Competition Commission of India (CCI).  
* Risks include reduced competition, higher prices for consumers, and potential for abuse by large companies acquiring distressed firms at the expense of smaller competitors.  
* The failing division defense applies to a failing part of a firm, not the entire entity. It faces a higher burden of proof due to potential manipulation to avoid competition scrutiny.  
* Countervailing buyer power refers to the strength of buyers to negotiate prices and conditions, acting as a check against the merged entity's market power.  
* The HHI measures market concentration. Comparing pre- and post-merger HHI values (the "delta") helps determine if the concentration change is significant enough to raise competition concerns.  
* Insolvency law prioritizes rescuing failing firms and maximizing creditor recovery, which might involve mergers increasing market concentration. Competition law aims to protect consumer welfare and market fairness, potentially conflicting with insolvency objectives.  
* The NCLAT, with expertise in both insolvency and competition law, could provide a more balanced review of IRPs, ensuring both creditor interests and market competition are adequately considered.

### **Glossary of Key Terms**

* **Insolvency Resolution Plan (IRP):** A plan submitted under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) outlining the restructuring or liquidation of a distressed company.  
* **Green-channeling:** A process that provides automatic approval to certain mergers or acquisitions without requiring a full competition assessment.  
* **Failing firm defense:** A legal defense used to justify a merger that would otherwise be anti-competitive, based on the argument that the target firm is failing and its exit would harm competition more than the merger.  
* **Suspensory merger control regime:** A system where mergers need to be cleared by the competition authority before they can be implemented.  
* **Voluntary merger control regime:** A system where mergers can be implemented but are subject to review and potential intervention by the competition authority after they have occurred.  
* **Appreciable Adverse Effect on Competition (AAEC):** A situation where a merger or acquisition is likely to significantly reduce competition in the market.  
* **Horizontal merger:** A merger between two companies operating in the same market and producing similar products or services.  
* **Vertical merger:** A merger between two companies operating at different stages of the same supply chain, such as a manufacturer and a retailer.  
* **Countervailing buyer power:** The ability of strong buyers to negotiate favorable prices and terms, acting as a constraint on the market power of sellers.  
* **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI):** A measure of market concentration, calculated by squaring the market shares of each firm in the market and summing the results.  
* **Committee of Creditors (CoC):** A committee formed under the IBC, consisting of financial creditors of the distressed company, responsible for approving or rejecting the IRP.  
* **National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT):** A quasi-judicial body in India responsible for adjudicating matters relating to company law, including insolvency proceedings.  
* **National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT):** An appellate tribunal in India that hears appeals from the NCLT.

# **Mergers of Distressed Firms: Frequently Asked Questions**

**1\. What is the 'failing firm defense' in competition law?**  
The failing firm defense allows competition authorities to approve mergers that would otherwise be anticompetitive if it can be proven that the target firm is failing and its exit from the market would be more detrimental than the merger itself. This defense acknowledges that allowing a failing firm to exit the market could lead to job losses, reduced consumer choice, and disruptions in the supply chain.  
**2\. How does the failing firm defense apply to mergers involving firms undergoing insolvency resolution processes (IRPs)?**  
Since firms undergoing IRPs are by definition financially distressed, they are often considered potential candidates for the failing firm defense. However, simply being in an IRP does not automatically qualify a merger for approval. Competition authorities still need to assess whether the conditions for the defense are met, including the imminent failure of the target firm and the lack of less anticompetitive alternatives.  
**3\. What are the criteria for establishing the failing firm defense in the European Union (EU)?**  
The EU has codified the failing firm defense in its Horizontal Merger Guidelines. To successfully invoke the defense, three tests must be satisfied:

* **Imminent Failure:** The target firm's business is likely to fail if the merger does not occur.  
* **No Less Anticompetitive Alternative:** There is no other acquirer available who would raise fewer competition concerns.  
* **Asset Exit:** The assets of the failing firm would exit the market in the absence of the merger.

**4\. How does the Competition Commission of India (CCI) assess mergers involving IRPs?**  
The CCI examines IRP mergers similarly to regular mergers, considering factors like horizontal or vertical overlaps, market shares, and the existence of countervailing factors like competition. While the failing firm defense is recognized in Indian law, the CCI has seldom explicitly used it in its decisions, choosing instead to focus on the overall impact of the merger on market competition.  
**5\. What are the potential conflicts between insolvency law and competition law in IRP mergers?**  
One major conflict arises from the competing priorities of creditors and competition. Creditors, primarily concerned with maximizing their recovery, might favor mergers with larger competitors who can offer higher bids. However, such mergers can significantly reduce competition, leading to higher prices and reduced consumer choice.  
**6\. What is the role of the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) in IRP mergers that have competition implications?**  
The NCLT approves IRPs and ensures they comply with existing laws, including competition law. Appeals against NCLT orders go to the NCLAT. In cases like the Binani Cement and Bhushan Power and Steel acquisitions, the NCLAT has favored bids that maximize the value for creditors, even if they raise competition concerns. This highlights the need for better coordination between insolvency and competition authorities.  
**7\. What are the potential risks and benefits of green-channelling IRP mergers in India?**  
Green-channelling, which grants automatic approval to mergers without scrutiny, could expedite the resolution process for distressed firms and attract more investors. However, the lack of scrutiny could lead to anticompetitive mergers slipping through, potentially harming consumers. The abuse of insolvency proceedings to bypass competition scrutiny is another concern.  
**8\. What measures can be taken to balance the objectives of competition law and insolvency law in IRP mergers?**  
Possible solutions include:

* A shorter, dedicated standstill period for IRP mergers, allowing for expedited scrutiny by the CCI.  
* Empowering the NCLAT to review IRP mergers for potential competition issues, leveraging its expertise in both insolvency and competition law.  
* Mandatory post-merger scrutiny by the CCI for green-channelled IRP mergers to identify and address anticompetitive outcomes.
