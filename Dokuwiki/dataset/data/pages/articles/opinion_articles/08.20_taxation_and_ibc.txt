# Taxation and IBC

This article examines the theoretical underpinnings of taxation and insolvency law in India, specifically focusing on the tension between these two fields within the context of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) of 2016\. It explores the historical development of taxation principles, analyzing them within two major theories of insolvency: the creditor's bargain theory and the communitarian theory. The article contrasts the two theories and their implications for the treatment of tax dues during insolvency proceedings, highlighting the inherent friction between them. It then examines the constitutional basis for taxation in India and how the evolution of jurisprudence has shaped the treatment of taxes in relation to trade and commerce. The authors conclude by emphasizing the need for a deeper understanding of the theoretical divergences between taxation and insolvency laws to create a consistent and efficient approach to tax treatment during insolvency resolution.

# **Taxation and Insolvency: An FAQ**

## **1\. What are the primary goals of modern insolvency law?**

Modern insolvency law aims to achieve several objectives, broadly categorized as follows:

* **Supporting the credit system**: It provides a framework for resolving financial distress in a predictable and fair manner, which encourages lending and economic activity.  
* **Early assessment and resolution**: It facilitates prompt identification and resolution of insolvency, minimizing losses and disruptions.  
* **Preventing creditor conflicts**: It establishes a collective procedure for resolving claims, avoiding chaotic individual actions by creditors.  
* **Value maximization and distribution**: It focuses on realizing the maximum value from the debtor's assets and distributing the proceeds fairly among creditors.  
* **Transparency and fairness**: It ensures transparent and honest proceedings, safeguarding the interests of all stakeholders.  
* **Identifying causes and preserving viable businesses**: It seeks to understand the reasons for insolvency and, where possible, preserve viable businesses to protect jobs and the economy.

## **2\. What is the Creditor's Bargain Theory, and how does it view taxation in insolvency?**

The Creditor's Bargain Theory posits that insolvency law should reflect a hypothetical agreement among creditors, made before any financial distress arises. It views insolvency resolution as a process driven by maximizing creditor wealth and emphasizes enforcing pre-insolvency contractual rights.  
Within this theory, taxation authorities are considered non-consensual creditors, meaning they are not party to the hypothetical "bargain" among creditors. Consequently, the theory provides no clear standing for tax claims in insolvency, and tax authorities would not be entitled to recover funds. Proponents argue that the state should adjust tax rates to account for potential losses from insolvency rather than prioritize tax claims in bankruptcy.

## **3\. What is the Communitarian Theory of insolvency, and how does it differ from the Creditor's Bargain Theory in its approach to taxation?**

The Communitarian Theory takes a broader perspective, viewing insolvency law as a public matter with implications for various stakeholders beyond just the creditors. It recognizes that corporations have responsibilities to employees, local communities, and society as a whole.  
Unlike the Creditor's Bargain Theory, the Communitarian Theory considers the interests of non-consensual creditors, including tax authorities, in insolvency proceedings. It acknowledges the importance of tax revenue for funding public services that benefit the community. This theory emphasizes achieving a fair distribution of resources, taking into account social and economic consequences, and promoting the rehabilitation of distressed companies where possible.

## **4\. What are the criticisms of the Creditor's Bargain Theory?**

The Creditor's Bargain Theory faces several criticisms, primarily:

* **Limited focus**: It prioritizes pre-insolvency rights and creditor wealth maximization, neglecting the interests of other stakeholders like employees and communities.  
* **Ignoring non-consensual creditors**: It fails to account for claims arising outside formal contractual agreements, leading to unfair outcomes for non-consensual creditors.  
* **Limited scope**: It struggles to address situations where a company's economic value doesn't exceed its liquidation value, hindering efforts to rehabilitate viable businesses.

## **5\. How does the Compensatory Tax Theory relate to insolvency?**

The Compensatory Tax Theory, previously prevalent in India but later rejected, argued that certain taxes levied in exchange for specific benefits provided by the state could be exempted from restrictions on trade and commerce.  
Applying this theory to insolvency would suggest that tax claims should be treated like unsecured debts since the state has provided services in exchange for those taxes. However, the theory's broad interpretation blurred the distinction between taxes and fees, leading to its rejection. The rejection re-emphasized the sovereign nature of taxation and its primary purpose of serving the public good.

## **6\. What is the constitutional basis of taxation in India, and how does it interact with insolvency law?**

The Constitution of India grants the state the sovereign right to levy taxes to raise revenue and fulfill public purposes. This right, enshrined in Article 366(28), has been upheld by the Supreme Court as essential for the government to function effectively.  
However, the tension arises when this sovereign right to taxation intersects with the objectives of insolvency law, particularly the focus on maximizing creditor recovery and rehabilitating viable businesses. Prioritizing tax claims in insolvency proceedings could potentially hinder these objectives.

## **7\. What are the arguments for and against prioritizing tax claims in insolvency proceedings?**

**Arguments for prioritizing tax claims:**

* **Public interest**: Tax revenue is essential for funding public services that benefit the community. Prioritizing tax claims ensures the government can fulfill its obligations.  
* **State sovereignty**: The state's right to collect taxes is a sovereign function, and this priority extends to insolvency proceedings.  
* **Fairness**: Businesses benefit from public services funded by taxes; therefore, they should contribute to those services even in insolvency.

**Arguments against prioritizing tax claims:**

* **Hinders rehabilitation**: Prioritizing tax claims could discourage stakeholders from investing in and reviving distressed businesses.  
* **Administrative burden**: It adds complexity to insolvency proceedings and increases the burden on already struggling companies.  
* **Potential abuse**: It might incentivize authorities to delay tax collection, hoping to benefit from higher priority in insolvency.

## **8\. How can the tension between taxation and insolvency be resolved?**

Addressing the conflict requires a nuanced approach that considers both the state's need for revenue and the objectives of insolvency law. Possible solutions include:

* **Improving tax administration**: Strengthening tax collection mechanisms can minimize revenue losses and reduce reliance on prioritizing tax claims in insolvency.  
* **Balancing creditor interests**: Developing a clear framework that balances the interests of different creditors, including tax authorities, secured creditors, and unsecured creditors, is crucial.  
* **Promoting rehabilitation**: Incentivizing the rescue of viable businesses should be a priority, with tax policies that support restructuring and continued operation.  
* **Clarity in legislation**: Clear and consistent legislation regarding the treatment of tax claims in insolvency proceedings is necessary to reduce uncertainty and litigation.

Ultimately, resolving the tension requires a pragmatic approach that considers the broader economic and social consequences, aiming to create a system that is both fair and efficient.

## **Cast of Characters**

**Philosophers/Economists:**

* **Adam Smith (1723-1790):** Scottish economist and philosopher. His book "The Wealth of Nations" laid out four maxims of taxation that greatly influenced modern tax systems, emphasizing fairness, certainty, convenience, and efficiency.  
* **Thomas Hobbes (1588-1679):** English philosopher known for his political philosophy, particularly his concept of the social contract. His work provided philosophical justification for state dominance in taxation and property rights, viewing taxes as the price for security and benefits provided by the sovereign.  
* **Charles Bastable (1855-1945):** Irish economist known for his work on public finance. He emphasized the importance of an elastic tax system, capable of adapting to economic changes and promoting economic growth without undue burdens.

**Legal Scholars:**

* **Douglas G. Baird:** American legal scholar specializing in bankruptcy law and a proponent of the Proceduralist perspective. He argues that insolvency law should focus on maximizing creditor wealth and that market forces should determine the fate of failing firms.

* **Sir Kenneth Cork (1911-1982):** British lawyer and judge. He chaired the 1982 Insolvency Law Review Committee, which produced the influential "Cork Report" advocating a rehabilitative approach to insolvency.  
* **Donald R. Korobkin:** American legal scholar known for his work on contract law and bankruptcy. A proponent of the Communitarian approach, he argues that insolvency law should consider a broader range of values and stakeholder interests, including employees, communities, and social welfare.
