# Related Party Veil

The provided excerpt from the paper "Rescuing Lenders: Lifting of the Related Party and Controlling Party Veil" by Saloni Thakkar, Urmika Tripathi and Simrann Venkkatesan argues that the Indian Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) needs clarification regarding the definition of "related party". The authors contend that the current definition can mistakenly classify pure-play creditors as related parties due to the common inclusion of lender protection rights in financing agreements. They highlight specific examples of lender rights, such as the right to appoint a nominee director, which could lead to this misclassification. The authors propose amending the IBC to exclude these typical lender rights from the "related party" definition to prevent unintended consequences for lenders and promote a predictable insolvency regime.

**Authors:** Saloni Thakkar, Urmika Tripathi, and Simrann Venkkatesan

**Main Themes:**

* The potential for unintended consequences for lenders under India's Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) due to a broad definition of "related party."  
* The impact of classifying lenders as related parties on their participation in the Committee of Creditors (CoC) and potential recoveries.  
* The ambiguity surrounding the status of assignees of related party debt and their eligibility to participate in the CoC.  
* Case laws highlighting the complexities of classifying lenders as related parties and the need for legislative clarity.

**Key Ideas and Facts:**

* The IBC introduced a "creditor-in-control" model with the CoC playing a crucial role in the insolvency resolution process.  
* **Section 21(2) of the IBC prevents related parties from representing, participating, or voting in the CoC.**  
* The definition of "related party" in **Section 5(24) is broad**, encompassing various relationships based on management powers, corporate structure, and voting rights.  
* **Typical lender protection rights**, such as appointing nominee directors or exercising veto rights, **can potentially classify lenders as related parties.**  
* **Being classified as a related party can have severe implications for lenders**:  
* **Exclusion from the CoC**, limiting their influence on the resolution process.  
* **Differential treatment in terms of recoveries**, potentially leading to lower payouts compared to unrelated creditors.  
* **Longer look-back period for avoidance transactions**, increasing scrutiny on past dealings with the debtor.  
* **Challenges in assigning related party debt**, potentially discouraging participation from other creditors.  
* **The ILC Report 2020 acknowledges these concerns and recommends broadening the exemption under Section 21 to accommodate legitimate lender rights.**  
* The status of assignees of related party debt remains unclear, with varying interpretations from Insolvency Tribunals.  
* **The ILC Report 2020 suggests allowing third-party assignees (unrelated to the debtor) to participate in the CoC**, while also cautioning against assignments made in bad faith to circumvent Section 21\.

**Important Quotes:**

* "However, other typical lender protection rights such as the right to appoint a nominee director, rights following invocation of shareholder pledge/s, lender protection veto rights on critical matters, have not been carved out and could arguably fall within the definition of a ‘related party’ under the IBC."  
* "The Insolvency Law Committee agreed that the scope of exemption under section 21 should be broadened to allow the Central Government “to prescribe additional transactions solely by completion of which a financial creditor which, is regulated by a financial sector regulator and not otherwise related to the corporate debtor, should not be considered as a ‘related party’ of the corporate debtor under this definition”."  
* "when a related party financial creditor assigns her debt to a third party in good faith, such third party should not be disqualified from participating, voting or being represented in a meeting of the CoC.”"

**Case Law Analysis:**

* **IDBI Trusteeship Services Ltd. v. Abhinav Mukherji:** NCLAT classified creditors as related parties due to their rights in the AoA, including appointing directors and influencing business plans. This highlights the potential for lender protection rights to trigger the related party definition.  
* **Housing & Urban Development Corporation Ltd.:** NCLT ruled that a lender with the right to appoint nominee directors was not a related party as the board did not act on their advice, demonstrating the importance of actual control.  
* **IFCI Limited v. Anshul Gupta:** NCLT recognized the need for flexibility in interpreting Section 21(2) and allowed a lender who became an equity shareholder due to the debtor's default to participate in the CoC.  
* **Phoenix Arc Pvt. Ltd. v. Spade Financial Services Ltd.:** Supreme Court clarified that the related party classification should be assessed "in praesenti" and that past relationships should not automatically disqualify creditors. However, the court also emphasized the need to prevent "commercial contrivances" aimed at manipulating the CoC composition.

**Suggested Amendments and Conclusion:**

* The authors advocate for clarifying the scope of "related party" provisions to focus on entities with *positive control* over the debtor, excluding impartial lenders exercising typical protection rights.  
* They highlight the need for predictable outcomes for lenders and avoiding unnecessary increases in the cost of credit.  
* The briefing concludes by emphasizing the importance of legislative clarity to ensure a balanced and effective insolvency regime in India.

# **FAQ: Related Party Veil in India's Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code**

## **1\. What is the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) of 2016 and why was it introduced?**

The IBC is a comprehensive legal framework in India that governs the insolvency resolution, reorganization, and liquidation of corporations, partnership firms, and individuals. It was introduced in 2016 to replace a fragmented system of insolvency laws and establish a more efficient and time-bound resolution process based on a "creditor-in-control" model.

## **2\. What is a Committee of Creditors (CoC) and what role does it play in the IBC?**

The CoC comprises the financial creditors of the distressed company (the Corporate Debtor or CD) undergoing insolvency resolution. The CoC holds significant decision-making powers, including approving the resolution plan, raising interim finance, and overseeing the resolution process.

## **3\. What does the IBC mean by "related party" and why are related parties excluded from the CoC?**

A "related party" under the IBC is broadly defined to encompass individuals or entities with close ties to the CD, such as directors, key managerial personnel, relatives, or entities with significant voting rights. Related parties are excluded from the CoC to prevent conflicts of interest and ensure that decision-making is impartial and focused on maximizing the value of the distressed company's assets for the benefit of all creditors.

## **4\. How can standard lender protection rights lead to a lender being classified as a related party?**

The IBC's broad definition of "related party" can inadvertently capture lenders who hold typical protective rights, such as the right to appoint a nominee director, veto rights on certain matters, or the ability to convert debt into equity. While these rights are intended to safeguard the lender's investment, they can be misconstrued as giving the lender control over the CD, leading to their classification as a related party.

## **5\. What are the consequences for a lender classified as a related party under the IBC?**

The primary consequence is exclusion from the CoC, depriving the lender of voting rights and participation in crucial decisions during the resolution process. Additionally, related party lenders may face differential treatment in terms of recoveries compared to other financial creditors, and transactions with them may be subject to longer look-back periods for scrutiny of potential avoidance actions.

## **6\. What are the controversies surrounding the classification of assignees of related party debt?**

The IBC is silent on the eligibility of third-party assignees of debt originally held by a related party to participate in the CoC. Conflicting judicial precedents exist, with some tribunals scrutinizing the assignment's validity and intent, while others automatically disqualify assignees based on the principle that they cannot have a better title than the original related party lender.

## **7\. What did the Supreme Court rule in the Phoenix Arc case regarding related party creditors and the CoC?**

In Phoenix Arc Pvt. Ltd. v. Spade Financial Services Ltd., the Supreme Court ruled that disqualification from the CoC applies only to creditors who are related parties "in praesenti," meaning at the time of the insolvency commencement date. However, the court also cautioned that if a related party divests its interest solely to circumvent the IBC's restrictions and influence the CoC, it can still be excluded to uphold the integrity of the resolution process.

## **8\. What amendments to the IBC are suggested to address the concerns regarding the treatment of lenders?**

To ensure fairness and predictability for lenders, the IBC should be amended to:

* **Clarify the scope of "related party"** to exclude impartial lenders who exercise typical protective rights without exerting positive control over the CD.  
* **Provide specific carve-outs** for common lender rights like nominee director appointments and veto rights.  
* **Establish clear guidelines** for determining the eligibility of assignees of related party debt to participate in the CoC, balancing the need to prevent abuse with the legitimate interests of bona fide assignees.

* **NCLAT decision in IDBI Trusteeship Services Ltd. v. Abhinav Mukherji:** The NCLAT rules that certain lender protection rights, such as the right to appoint directors and veto power, can classify a lender as a "related party." The Supreme Court issues a status quo order pending appeal.  
* **NCLAT decision in Citi Securities & Financial Services v. Sudip Bhatacharya:** The NCLAT upholds the classification of an assignee of related party debt as a related party, citing concerns about backdoor entry into the CoC and lack of good faith in the assignment.

* **NCLAT decision in Rare Reconstruction Ltd. v. Avishek Gupta:** The NCLAT upholds the classification of an assignee of related party debt as a related party based on the circumstances of the assignment and concerns about potential influence over the CIRP.

**1\. Financial Creditors (FCs):** These are entities who have lent money to the Corporate Debtor (CD), typically banks, financial institutions, and debenture holders. The IBC grants them significant control in the CIRP through the CoC.  
**2\. Corporate Debtor (CD):** This is the company undergoing insolvency proceedings under the IBC.  
**3\. Resolution Professional (RP):** This is an insolvency professional appointed to manage the CD during the CIRP. The RP has the powers of the CD's board but requires CoC approval for key decisions.  
**4\. Committee of Creditors (CoC):** This committee is composed of the CD's FCs and plays a crucial role in the CIRP. The CoC approves the resolution plan, can raise interim finance, and has other significant powers.  
**5\. Related Party:** The IBC defines a "related party" to a CD broadly, including individuals and entities with significant influence over the CD, such as directors, key managerial personnel, and those with substantial voting rights. Related party FCs are excluded from the CoC.
