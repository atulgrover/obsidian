The paper provides a detailed analysis of the obstacles faced by OCs, including limited participation in the decision-making process, challenges in verifying claims, and low priority in the payment hierarchy. The authors conclude by emphasizing the need for a more equitable and inclusive insolvency resolution framework that adequately addresses the concerns and interests of OCs.

# **FAQ: The Plight of Operational Creditors in the Indian Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code**

## **1\. What is the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) of 2016, and what are its main objectives?**

The IBC is a landmark reform in the Indian economy, aiming to streamline insolvency and bankruptcy proceedings. Its primary objectives are to:

* **Rehabilitate viable distressed companies**: This includes restructuring debt, bringing in new management, or merging with healthier entities.  
* **Ensure fair and equitable treatment to all stakeholders**: This includes financial creditors, operational creditors, employees, and the debtor company itself.

However, the practical implementation of the IBC has raised concerns about the treatment of operational creditors.

## **2\. Who are Operational Creditors (OCs) under the IBC, and how do they differ from Financial Creditors (FCs)?**

The IBC classifies creditors into two main categories:

* **Financial Creditors (FCs):** Typically banks and financial institutions that lend money to companies. Their debts are usually secured against assets and have specific repayment timelines.  
* **Operational Creditors (OCs):** Entities providing goods and services to a company in its day-to-day operations. This includes suppliers, vendors, employees, and government dues for taxes and utilities. OCs' debts are generally unsecured and lack strict repayment schedules.

## **3\. What are the main challenges faced by OCs during insolvency proceedings under the IBC?**

OCs face several disadvantages and obstacles during insolvency proceedings:

* **Limited Influence:** OCs have limited representation and voting rights in the Committee of Creditors (CoC), the body that makes key decisions during insolvency proceedings. The CoC is predominantly composed of FCs.  
* **Information Asymmetry:** OCs often receive inadequate and untimely information about the insolvency process, hindering their ability to participate effectively.  
* **Claims Verification:** The process for verifying claims can be complex and burdensome for OCs, particularly due to the unsecured nature of their debts and often less stringent documentation practices.  
* **Low Priority in Payment:** The IBC's waterfall mechanism prioritizes secured creditors and FCs in the distribution of proceeds from the sale of assets during liquidation. OCs are often left with minimal recovery, sometimes receiving haircuts of 95% or more on their dues.

## **4\. How does the limited representation of OCs in the CoC impact their interests?**

The CoC holds significant power in insolvency proceedings, making decisions about the appointment of the Resolution Professional, the approval of the resolution plan, and the liquidation process. The lack of voting rights and limited representation for OCs within the CoC can lead to:

* **Resolution Plans Favoring FCs:** Resolution plans, primarily driven by FCs, often offer substantial haircuts to OCs, prioritizing the recovery of FCs' dues.  
* **Ignoring OCs Concerns:** Key decisions may not adequately consider the unique challenges and interests of OCs, leading to outcomes that disproportionately benefit FCs.

## **5\. Are there any legal precedents that highlight the issues faced by OCs in IBC proceedings?**

Yes, several judicial pronouncements illustrate the challenges faced by OCs:

* **Genius Security and Allied Services v. Mr. Shivadutt Bannanje:** The NCLAT upheld a resolution plan that offered no payment to OCs, arguing that they would have received nothing in liquidation anyway.  
* **Damodar Valley Corporation v. Dimension Steel Alloys:** The NCLAT acknowledged the need for equitable treatment of creditors after an OC appealed against a resolution plan that offered them only a meager 0.19% of their admitted dues.

These cases demonstrate the vulnerability of OCs and the need for a more balanced approach that protects their interests.

## **6\. What are the broader economic consequences of the current treatment of OCs under the IBC?**

The current framework's limitations in safeguarding OCs' interests can have adverse economic consequences:

* **Discouraging Trade Credit:** The fear of significant losses during insolvency can discourage businesses from extending trade credit, potentially hampering economic activity.  
* **Cascading Impact on MSMEs:** Many OCs are Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs). Significant haircuts for them during insolvency proceedings can lead to financial distress, potentially causing further bankruptcies and job losses.  
* **Eroding Trust in the Credit System:** The perception of an uneven playing field between FCs and OCs can erode trust in the overall credit system, making it harder for businesses to access necessary funding.

## **7\. What are some potential solutions to address the concerns of OCs in IBC proceedings?**

Several reforms could help improve the position of OCs:

* **Enhanced Representation in the CoC:** Granting OCs greater representation and voting rights in the CoC would ensure their concerns are adequately considered during decision-making.  
* **Improved Information Flow:** Establishing mechanisms for timely and transparent information dissemination to all creditors, including OCs, would enable them to participate more effectively in the process.  
* **Streamlined Claims Verification:** Simplifying the claims verification process, particularly for OCs, would reduce administrative burden and expedite the resolution process.  
* **Addressing Payment Priority:** Revisiting the waterfall mechanism to ensure a more equitable distribution of proceeds during liquidation, particularly for smaller OCs and essential service providers.

## **8\. What steps can OCs take to protect their interests during insolvency proceedings?**

OCs can take proactive steps to safeguard their interests:

* **Maintain Meticulous Records:** Thorough documentation of transactions, invoices, and contracts is crucial for effective claims verification.  
* **Seek Expert Advice:** Consulting with legal and financial professionals specializing in insolvency proceedings can help OCs navigate the complexities of the process.  
* **Engage in Collective Action:** OCs can form associations or groups to advocate for their interests and present a united front during negotiations and legal proceedings.

By understanding their rights and actively participating in the process, OCs can work towards achieving a fairer outcome in insolvency situations.

### **Glossary of Key Terms:**

**Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC):** A comprehensive legislation in India that governs insolvency and bankruptcy proceedings for companies, partnerships, and individuals. Its objective is to consolidate and streamline the insolvency process, ensuring a time-bound resolution for distressed entities and maximizing value for stakeholders.  
**Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP):** A formal process initiated under the IBC when a company defaults on its debt obligations. It involves a structured framework for resolving the insolvency of a company within a specified timeframe, either through a resolution plan or liquidation.  
**Financial Creditor (FC):** A creditor whose relationship with the debtor arises from a financial transaction, typically involving lending or extending credit. Examples include banks, financial institutions, and bondholders.  
**Operational Creditor (OC):** A creditor whose relationship with the debtor arises from providing goods or services in the ordinary course of business. Examples include suppliers, service providers, and employees.  
**Committee of Creditors (CoC):** A committee formed under the IBC consisting exclusively of financial creditors. The CoC has significant decision-making power in the CIRP, including approving the resolution plan and deciding on the liquidation of the company.  
**Resolution Plan:** A comprehensive plan proposed during the CIRP outlining the strategies for reviving the financially distressed company and repaying its creditors. The resolution plan needs approval from the CoC and the Adjudicating Authority.  
**Liquidation:** A process initiated under the IBC when the resolution of a company's insolvency through a resolution plan is not feasible. Liquidation involves the sale of the company's assets and the distribution of the proceeds among creditors according to a specified priority.  
**Waterfall Mechanism:** A hierarchical framework defined in Section 53 of the IBC that dictates the order of priority in which creditors receive payment from the proceeds of the liquidation process.  
**Haircut:** The reduction in the amount a creditor agrees to accept as a settlement compared to the original claim amount. Haircuts are often part of resolution plans in CIRP to achieve a viable solution for the distressed company.  
**Adjudicating Authority (AA):** The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) is designated as the Adjudicating Authority for corporate insolvency cases under the IBC. It is responsible for admitting insolvency applications, overseeing the CIRP, and approving resolution plans or ordering liquidation.
