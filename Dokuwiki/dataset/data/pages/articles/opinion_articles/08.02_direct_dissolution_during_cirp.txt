# **FAQ: Direct Dissolution During CIRP in India**

## **1\. What is the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) of 2016?**

The IBC is a comprehensive legal framework in India designed to resolve insolvency and bankruptcy issues efficiently. It aims to maximize asset value, promote entrepreneurship, enhance credit availability, and balance stakeholders' interests. The Code prioritizes resolution over recovery and features a time-bound process to preserve the economic value of distressed businesses.

## **2\. What is the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP)?**

CIRP is a process under the IBC that attempts to revive a financially distressed company. It involves assessing the company's viability, negotiating between creditors and debtors, and developing a resolution plan. Creditors have the final say in the viability of the business and the resolution plan.

## **3\. What happens if the CIRP fails?**

If no resolution plan is approved or implemented during the CIRP, the company is ordered into liquidation. The assets are sold, and proceeds are distributed to creditors according to a prescribed waterfall mechanism. After the liquidation process, the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) orders the company's dissolution.

## **4\. What is "Direct Dissolution," and why is it being considered?**

Direct dissolution refers to dissolving a company during the CIRP without going through the liquidation process. This is proposed for cases where the company has no assets to realize. Proponents argue that mandating liquidation in such scenarios leads to unnecessary delays, costs, and burdens the judicial system.

## **5\. What are the benefits of allowing Direct Dissolution?**

Direct dissolution offers several advantages:

* **Cost efficiency:** Eliminates liquidation costs like liquidator fees and administrative expenses, which are wasted when no assets exist.  
* **Time efficiency:** Expedites the closure of the insolvency process, freeing up resources for stakeholders.  
* **Reduced judicial burden:** Reduces the workload of NCLT, allowing them to focus on cases with potential asset recovery.  
* **Alignment with IBC objectives:** Reflects the Code's emphasis on resolution and avoids unnecessary formalities when liquidation is guaranteed to be ineffective.

## **6\. Are there any safeguards against misuse of Direct Dissolution?**

Yes, several safeguards are proposed to prevent abuse:

* **Verification of nil assets:** Thorough verification by the Resolution Professional (RP), potentially including a certificate from a Registered Valuer, to confirm the absence of assets.  
* **Mandatory CoC approval:** The Committee of Creditors (CoC) must approve the direct dissolution, ensuring no stakeholder is disadvantaged. The CoC must also document the reasons for their decision.  
* **Judicial oversight:** The NCLT retains oversight to ensure the decision is justified and considers all stakeholders' interests.

## **7\. Are there any precedents for Direct Dissolution in India?**

The NCLT has ordered direct dissolution in several cases where companies had no realizable assets. These decisions, along with observations from the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT), set a precedent for this approach.

## **8\. What is the proposed way forward for implementing Direct Dissolution?**

Experts suggest amending the IBC and its regulations to formally allow direct dissolution after a failed CIRP for companies with no assets. This could involve introducing a standardized procedure with mandatory conditions and a compliance checklist for clarity and transparency. This would provide clear guidance to all stakeholders and help ensure consistency in applying the provision.
