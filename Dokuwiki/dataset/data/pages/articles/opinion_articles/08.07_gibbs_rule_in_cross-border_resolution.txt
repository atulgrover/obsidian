# Gibbs Rule in Cross Border Resolution

The article argues that the Gibbs Rule, a century-old English legal principle, is out of touch with the modern, globalized landscape of business and cross-border insolvency. The authors contrast the Gibbs Rule with the modified universalism approach, which emphasizes cooperation and coordination between jurisdictions. They examine India's draft legislation on cross-border insolvency, which seeks to incorporate the UNCITRAL Model Law and move toward a modified universalism approach. The authors highlight the shortcomings of the current Indian legal framework and the need for a more comprehensive approach to recognize and enforce foreign insolvency-related judgments. They use the Jet Airways case as an example of how modified universalism can be applied in practice, demonstrating its advantages in navigating concurrent insolvency proceedings in different countries.

**I. Introduction to Cross-Border Insolvency**  
This section provides a brief overview of cross-border insolvency, emphasizing the challenges posed by conflicting legal systems and the increasing inadequacy of traditional insolvency proceedings in a globalized economy. It highlights the need for robust frameworks that recognize foreign proceedings, facilitate coordination, and foster communication among jurisdictions.

**II. Historical Background of the Gibbs Rule**

* **A. Origins and Development**  
  This subsection delves into the origins of the Gibbs Rule, established in the 1890 case *Antony Gibbs & Sons v. La Société Industrielle et Commerciale des Métaux*. It explains the case facts and the rationale behind Lord Escher MR’s decision, which established that discharge of debt in a foreign jurisdiction is valid in England only if it aligns with the governing law of the contract.  
* **B. Examination of the Legal and Economic Landscape at the Time**  
  This subsection contextualizes the formulation of the Gibbs Rule within the legal and economic climate of the late 19th century. It contrasts the limited cross-border trade of that era with the complexities of modern multinational corporations and globalized commerce, highlighting the inadequacy of the Gibbs Rule in contemporary contexts.

**III. Critique of the Gibbs Rule**

* **A. Straying Away from the Principle of Collectivity**  
  This subsection critiques the Gibbs Rule for prioritizing contractual agreements over the collective interests of creditors in insolvency proceedings. It argues that the rule undermines the principle of collectivity, which seeks to distribute a debtor's remaining assets equitably among all creditors, regardless of individual contractual terms.  
* **B. Inconsistency Arising From an Application of Gibbs**  
  This subsection examines the inconsistencies that arise when applying the Gibbs Rule alongside other private international law rules within English insolvency law. It cites specific examples of how recognizing foreign proceedings while simultaneously upholding the Gibbs Rule can create legal contradictions and potentially unjust situations for debtors and creditors.  
* **C. Failure to Address the Globalized Nature of Modern Businesses**  
  * **1\. Multinational Corporations and Their Impact on Insolvency \-** This sub-section argues that the Gibbs Rule is ill-suited to address the complexities of modern multinational corporations operating across multiple jurisdictions. Its rigid focus on contractual obligations fails to consider the interconnectedness of assets and creditors, leading to inefficiencies and potential value erosion for stakeholders.  
  * **2\. Global Distribution of Creditors and Stakeholders \-** This sub-section emphasizes that the Gibbs Rule neglects the global distribution of creditors and stakeholders in contemporary insolvency cases. Its emphasis on traditional contractual obligations hinders effective communication and collaboration among international participants, leading to challenges in enforcing and recognizing foreign insolvency proceedings.

**IV. The Shift to Modified Universalism**

* **A. Introduction to Modified Universalism \-** This subsection introduces the concept of Modified Universalism, which promotes cooperation and coordination in cross-border insolvency proceedings. It contrasts Modified Universalism with Pure Universalism, explaining its more pragmatic approach that balances international cooperation with the preservation of local interests and creditor rights within individual jurisdictions.

The conclusion reiterates the advantages of Modified Universalism over the Gibbs Rule, emphasizing its ability to address the complexities of modern cross-border insolvency cases. * **Agrokor d.d. restructuring case.** US Bankruptcy Court grants Chapter 15 recognition to Croatian restructuring proceedings, explicitly favoring modified universalism over the Gibbs Rule, and facilitating US enforcement of non-US restructuring for English-law-governed debt.

## Evaluating the Archaic Nature of the ‘Gibbs Rule’ in Cross-Border Insolvency

**Main Themes:**

1. **Critique of the Gibbs Rule:** This article argues that the Gibbs Rule, a longstanding principle in English common law governing cross-border insolvency, is outdated and ill-suited to the realities of modern globalized business.  
2. **Modified Universalism as a Solution:** The authors advocate for replacing the Gibbs Rule with a modified universalism approach, which emphasizes international cooperation and coordination in cross-border insolvency cases.  
3. **India's Move Towards Modified Universalism:** The article highlights India's efforts to incorporate the UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency into its domestic legislation, demonstrating a shift towards a modified universalism approach.

**Most Important Ideas/Facts:**

1. **Origins and Limitations of the Gibbs Rule:**  
* Formulated in 1890, the Gibbs Rule dictates that the discharge of debt in a foreign insolvency proceeding is recognized in England only if it aligns with the governing law of the contract.  
* The rationale is that parties to a contract should not be bound by the insolvency laws of a jurisdiction they didn't agree to.  
* Critics argue that this approach prioritizes contractual rights over the broader policy considerations of insolvency, undermining the principle of collective action in bankruptcy proceedings.  
* "The Gibbs Rule assumes (perhaps rightly at the time it was decided, but not applicable in the present day) that a debtor borrows in only one market, under one law, and therefore, assigns primacy to the choice of law of the contract governing the debt over the subject matter jurisdiction of the insolvency court."  
2. **Inconsistency and Injustice of the Gibbs Rule:**  
* Application of the Gibbs Rule can lead to situations where a debtor is stripped of assets through recognition of foreign insolvency proceedings in England but remains liable for debts under those same proceedings.  
* This creates a scenario where "what English insolvency law gives with one hand, it takes away with the other."  
3. **Inadequacy in Addressing Globalized Business:**  
* The Gibbs Rule, conceived in an era of limited cross-border trade, struggles to address the complexities of modern multinational corporations operating across multiple jurisdictions.  
* It fails to adequately consider the global distribution of creditors and stakeholders, potentially leading to inefficiencies, increased costs, and delays in resolving cross-border insolvencies.  
4. **Benefits of Modified Universalism:**  
* This approach promotes cooperation and coordination among jurisdictions in cross-border insolvency cases.  
* It aims to achieve fair and efficient outcomes by recognizing foreign proceedings, facilitating communication among stakeholders, and prioritizing the equitable distribution of assets.  
* Key cases like *HIH Insurance* and *Cambridge Gas* showcase successful implementations of modified universalism principles.  
5. **India's Shift Towards Modified Universalism:**  
* The current Indian insolvency law (IBC) contains limited provisions for cross-border insolvency, primarily focused on reciprocity and letters of request.  
* To address this, the Insolvency Law Committee has recommended incorporating the UNCITRAL Model Law, with modifications, into the IBC.  
* This signifies a move away from the territorial approach towards modified universalism, aligning India's insolvency framework with international best practices.  
6. **Limitations of the UNCITRAL Model Law:**  
* While the Model Law fosters cooperation and recognition of foreign insolvency proceedings, it doesn't explicitly provide for the enforcement of foreign insolvency judgments.  
* This gap was highlighted in the case of *Re OJSC International Bank of Azerbaijan,* where the English Court of Appeal declined to permanently stay enforcement actions despite recognizing the foreign insolvency proceedings.  
* To address this deficiency, the UNCITRAL introduced the Model Law on the Recognition and Enforcement of Insolvency-Related Judgments (MLREIJ).  
7. **India's Future Direction:**  
* While India's proposed adoption of the Model Law represents a positive step, consideration should be given to incorporating the MLREIJ for a comprehensive framework for cross-border insolvency.  
* Such an approach would contribute to a more predictable and investor-friendly environment, further aligning India's insolvency regime with the demands of a globalized economy.

**Conclusion:**  
This article argues convincingly that the Gibbs Rule is outdated and ill-equipped to handle the complexities of cross-border insolvency in a globalized world. Modified universalism offers a more practical and equitable approach, fostering international cooperation, and promoting fair outcomes for all stakeholders. India's move towards embracing modified universalism through proposed amendments to the IBC is a positive development, signaling the country's commitment to a more modern and internationally aligned insolvency regime.

### **Glossary of Key Terms**

1. **Gibbs Rule:** A common law principle stating that a discharge of debt under foreign bankruptcy law is only valid in England if it aligns with the governing law of the contract.  
2. **Cross-border Insolvency:** Insolvency cases involving debtors with assets, creditors, or operations in multiple jurisdictions.  
3. **Territorialism:** An approach to insolvency where each jurisdiction applies its own laws and procedures, often leading to conflicts and inefficiencies in cross-border cases.  
4. **Modified Universalism:** A cooperative approach to cross-border insolvency that recognizes a primary proceeding in the debtor's main jurisdiction while allowing for ancillary proceedings in other jurisdictions where assets or creditors are located.  
5. **Pure Universalism:** A hypothetical approach envisioning a single global insolvency framework, which is currently impractical due to variations in legal systems and national interests.  
6. **Centre of Main Interests (COMI):** The jurisdiction where the debtor's main business activities are located, often used to determine the primary insolvency proceeding.  
7. **Model Law:** The UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency, providing a framework for recognition and cooperation in cross-border insolvency cases.  
8. **MLREIJ:** The Model Law on Recognition and Enforcement of Insolvency-Related Judgments, complementing the Model Law by addressing the recognition and enforcement of foreign insolvency judgments.  
9. **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC):** India's primary legislation governing insolvency and bankruptcy proceedings.  
10. **National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT):** The adjudicating authority in India for insolvency cases under the IBC.  
11. **Foreign Main Proceeding:** An insolvency proceeding in the jurisdiction where the debtor's COMI is located.  
12. **Foreign Non-Main Proceeding:** An insolvency proceeding in a jurisdiction other than the debtor's COMI, typically where the debtor has an establishment.

# FAQ: Cross-Border Insolvency and the Gibbs Rule

1. ### What is cross-border insolvency?

   1. Cross-border insolvency refers to situations where a financially distressed debtor, typically a company, has assets or operations in multiple jurisdictions. These cases require harmonizing diverse legal regimes to ensure a fair and efficient resolution process for all stakeholders involved. This complexity stems from conflicting laws, varying judicial systems, and diverse socio-economic environments.

2. ### What is the Gibbs Rule and why is it considered outdated?

   1. The Gibbs Rule, originating from an 1890 English court case, states that the discharge of a debt in a foreign insolvency proceeding is recognized in England only if it aligns with the governing law of the contract that created the debt. Critics argue that this principle, rooted in a bygone era of limited cross-border transactions, fails to address the realities of modern globalized businesses and leads to inefficiencies and inequities in resolving complex multinational insolvencies.

3. ### How does the Gibbs Rule hinder the Principle of Collectivity in insolvency cases?

   1. The Gibbs Rule, by prioritizing contractual obligations over insolvency principles, undermines the Principle of Collectivity, a cornerstone of modern insolvency law. This principle emphasizes treating all creditors fairly and proportionally in distributing a debtor's assets. Gibbs can allow certain creditors to circumvent the collective process by enforcing claims based on the governing law of their contract, even if it contradicts a foreign court-approved restructuring plan.

4. ### What is Modified Universalism and how does it improve upon the Gibbs Rule?

   1. Modified Universalism represents a more modern and collaborative approach to cross-border insolvency. It advocates for recognizing and cooperating with foreign insolvency proceedings while allowing for some flexibility to protect local creditors' interests. This approach favors a primary insolvency proceeding in the debtor's main jurisdiction, supported by ancillary proceedings in other countries where assets are located. This collaborative framework promotes efficiency, fairness, and predictability in resolving complex international insolvencies.

5. ### How do landmark cases like HIH Insurance and Agrokor demonstrate the shift toward Modified Universalism?

   1. The HIH Insurance case in Australia marked a departure from strict territorialism by recognizing the importance of international cooperation. The court recognized the validity of foreign insolvency proceedings and aimed for equitable distribution of assets to creditors globally. Similarly, in the Agrokor case, a US court recognized the Croatian restructuring proceedings, critiquing the Gibbs Rule for its inconsistency with modern international insolvency law principles and the Model Law, which champions Modified Universalism.

6. ### What steps has India taken to integrate the Model Law and embrace Modified Universalism?

   1. India, recognizing the limitations of its existing cross-border insolvency framework, has proposed Draft Provisions based on the UNCITRAL Model Law. These provisions advocate for reciprocity, allowing for the recognition of foreign proceedings and cooperation between courts and insolvency professionals in different jurisdictions. The Draft Provisions also aim to establish a clear process for coordinating concurrent insolvency proceedings in multiple countries, aligning with the principles of Modified Universalism.

7. ### Does the Model Law address the enforcement of foreign insolvency-related judgments?

   1. While the Model Law focuses on the recognition and cooperation in insolvency proceedings, it lacks a standardized framework for enforcing foreign judgments resulting from these processes. This deficiency is evident in cases like Re OJSC International Bank of Azerbaijan, where the English Court of Appeal, while recognizing the Azeri insolvency proceeding, could not enforce its outcome due to the constraints of the Gibbs Rule and the absence of a mechanism for recognizing foreign judgments.

8. ### How does the Jet Airways case illustrate the practical application of Modified Universalism in India?

   1. The Jet Airways case exemplifies the judicial implementation of Modified Universalism principles in India, even in the absence of a dedicated legal framework. The courts, recognizing concurrent insolvency proceedings in India and the Netherlands, facilitated the development of the Jet Protocol. This agreement established India as the primary jurisdiction and ensured cooperation between the insolvency professionals in both countries, promoting efficient and coordinated resolution of the case.
